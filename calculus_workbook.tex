\chapter*{About this book}

Knowing the rules of calculus and being able to carry a difficult calculation
through to completion are different accomplishments. A familiar derivative can
be hidden inside an unfamiliar expression. A promising substitution can fail at
a turning point. Several pages of correct algebra can still lead to a result
that is valid on only part of the original domain. These are the difficulties
this workbook is intended to exercise.

The reader is assumed to have studied calculus. The aim is sustained practice:
choosing a way into a problem, developing it far enough to see whether it works,
and accounting for the assumptions on which the result depends. Some solutions
become short after a useful reformulation. Others remain long. Both kinds belong
here. A complicated answer is acceptable when the calculation is sound and its
meaning is clear.

Notation provides the common language for that work. The opening chapter records
the conventions used throughout the book: what each quantity represents, which
variables it depends on, and precisely which operation is being performed. Full
function arguments and explicit derivative operators reduce the amount that has
to be reconstructed from context. The methods reference that follows collects
formulas and their conditions in one place, so a calculation can be resumed
without relying on perfect recall.

The main chapters contain mixed problems. A heading identifies the mathematical
setting, but the choice of method is part of the problem. The worked solutions
explain what made an approach promising, carry out the intermediate calculations,
and examine the places where that approach could have failed. Algebra appears
in dark blue so that a sign, coefficient, or cancellation can be checked without
losing the thread of the argument. Domain discussions are marked in amber;
supporting identities and approximations appear in purple. The labels remain
useful in a monochrome printout.

Each worked problem in the printed edition begins with its complete statement
and continues directly into the solution. The problem identifiers and numbered
steps make particular calculations easy to find again. Hints are collected in
a separate appendix for readers who want a smaller amount of help first. On the
website, the hints and solutions can be opened individually. Simpler reference
exercises are retained in the appendices when a local calculation needs a more
familiar example.

The purpose of the answers is to make independent work possible, including the
part of that work that involves becoming stuck. Consulting an identity, comparing
two representations, or returning to an earlier line is part of solving a
problem. Over time, repeated encounters with those decisions build a practical
sense of which approaches are likely to succeed and which conditions deserve
closer attention.

\chapter{Notational conventions}

A mathematical expression is easier to follow when its notation carries the
information needed to interpret it. In this book, function arguments remain
visible, the variable of each operation is named, and the types of the quantities
involved are stated alongside their definitions. The conventions are drawn from
established calculus and linear algebra notation. Where several conventions are
common, one is used consistently here. Their sources and the reasons for these
choices are discussed in the notation review.

\section{1. Objects, domains, and dependencies}

Derivatives are written as Leibniz operators followed by a parenthesized
expression. The operator names the differentiation variable; the expression
shows the function and its arguments. This makes a derivative readable without
having to recover an omitted dependency from an earlier paragraph.

A problem statement establishes four aspects of the calculation:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Objects and types.} A quantity may be a scalar, a vector of a stated dimension, a matrix of a stated shape, or a tensor whose space, basis, and component indices have been specified.
\item \textbf{Dependencies.} Independent variables are distinguished from dependent quantities and fixed parameters. Intermediate functions are named when they make a long expression easier to follow.
\item \textbf{Domains and assumptions.} The admissible inputs and excluded values are stated, together with relevant sign or regularity conditions.
\item \textbf{Operations and outputs.} The variable of differentiation or integration is identified, as are the quantities held fixed and the type of the result.

\end{enumerate}
The same information is repeated in a solution when it is needed to follow a
particular step. A vector or matrix can serve as one function argument once its
components and dependencies have been defined. This keeps the expressions
manageable while preserving the distinction between a scalar input and a
collection of inputs.

\subsection{Function arguments and fixed parameters}

A function and a value of that function are distinct objects. The symbol \(f\)
denotes a function; \(f(x,y)\) denotes its value at the displayed inputs.
Accordingly, a dependent scalar output is written as \(z=f(x,y)\) rather than
\(z=f\).

Fixed parameters are separated from variable inputs by a semicolon. For
example, in the real scalar function \(f(x;a)=ax^2\), \(x\) is an independent real
scalar input and \(a\) is a fixed real scalar parameter. The semicolon records
this distinction; it does not denote an additional calculus operation.
When a quantity varies with an input, its dependence is displayed explicitly,
as in \(a(x)\).

For a real scalar function \(z=f(x,y)\) of two independent real scalar
coordinates, \(x\) and \(y\) vary independently. A dependence on a single path
parameter is represented instead by

\[
t\longmapsto (x(t),y(t))\longmapsto z(t)=f(x(t),y(t)).
\]

Here \(t\) is the independent real scalar parameter; \(x(t)\) and \(y(t)\) are
dependent real scalar coordinates; and \(z(t)\) is the dependent real scalar
output. The parenthesized coordinate pair represents a point and is
separate from the bracketed column-vector representation.

\section{3. Derivatives}

\subsection{One independent scalar variable}

For a differentiable real scalar-valued function \(f(x)\) of an independent
real scalar input \(x\), the derivative is denoted by

\[
\frac{d}{dx}\left(f(x)\right).
\]

The operator \(d/dx\) denotes differentiation with respect to \(x\); the
parenthesized expression is its operand. The differentiation variable and
the function's input are therefore visible in the same expression. Prime
marks and time dots are replaced by this operator notation in worked
calculations. An additional pair of parentheses around the operator itself
is not part of the adopted convention.

The second derivative is represented by the established higher-order
operator, whose repeated-operation meaning is

\[
\frac{d^2}{dx^2}\left(f(x)\right)
=\frac{d}{dx}\left(\frac{d}{dx}\left(f(x)\right)\right).
\]

The superscript \(2\) indicates two successive differentiations rather than
the square of the derivative. Intermediate derivatives are displayed in
worked higher-order calculations.

Evaluation of the derivative at a fixed real scalar input \(a\) is denoted by

\[
\left.\frac{d}{dx}\left(f(x)\right)\right|_{x=a}.
\]

The vertical bar denotes evaluation of the differentiated expression.
Differentiation precedes substitution of the specified input. This
operation differs from differentiation of the constant value \(f(a)\).

\subsection{Several independent scalar variables}

For a differentiable real scalar function \(f(x,y)\) of independent real
scalar inputs \(x,y\), partial differentiation is written as

\[
\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\left(f(x,y)\right),
\qquad y\text{ held fixed}.
\]

The variable of differentiation is given in the denominator of the
operator. The other independent inputs and fixed parameters are identified
in accompanying text. Where different choices of fixed quantities lead to
different results, the held-fixed quantities are stated with each relevant
expression. A subscripted evaluation bar is reserved for evaluation and is
not also used to denote a held-fixed quantity.

The symbols \(d\) and \(\partial\) distinguish ordinary and partial
differentiation. In a partial derivative, one independent coordinate varies
while the others are held fixed. Ordinary differentiation applies to an
expression with one independent scalar input, including a composition
whose coordinates depend on a single path parameter.

For a differentiable real scalar function \(f(x,y)\) and differentiable real
scalar paths \(x(t),y(t)\) with independent real scalar parameter \(t\), the
path derivative is represented by

\[
\begin{aligned}
\frac{d}{dt}\left(f(x(t),y(t))\right)
={}&\left.\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\left(f(x,y)\right)
\right|_{x=x(t),\,y=y(t)}
\frac{d}{dt}\left(x(t)\right)\\
&+\left.\frac{\partial}{\partial y}\left(f(x,y)\right)
\right|_{x=x(t),\,y=y(t)}
\frac{d}{dt}\left(y(t)\right).
\end{aligned}
\]

In the first partial derivative \(y\) is held fixed; in the second \(x\) is
held fixed. Each partial derivative is formed in the independent
coordinates, evaluated on the path, and multiplied by the corresponding
coordinate rate. The resulting path derivative is scalar-valued.

Mixed partial derivatives are initially represented as nested operations,
with their order stated in words. Equality after reversal of that order
requires appropriate hypotheses and is not implied by the notation.

\subsection{Vector and matrix outputs}

For a single scalar input, differentiation of vector components is
performed in a fixed basis. For example, \(t\) is an independent real scalar,
\(\mathbf r(t)\) is a two-component real column vector, and \(r_1(t),r_2(t)\)
are differentiable real scalar component functions:

\[
\mathbf r(t)=\begin{bmatrix}r_1(t)\\r_2(t)\end{bmatrix},
\qquad
\frac{d}{dt}\left(\mathbf r(t)\right)
=\begin{bmatrix}
\frac{d}{dt}\left(r_1(t)\right)\\
\frac{d}{dt}\left(r_2(t)\right)
\end{bmatrix}.
\]

The derivative is a column vector with the same number of components.
Round derivative-operand delimiters are thereby distinguished from square
vector-display delimiters. Matrix and tensor derivatives are accompanied
by the corresponding shape declarations.

For a differentiable real vector-valued function
\(\mathbf F(x_1,\ldots,x_n)\in\mathbb R^m\) of independent real scalar
coordinates \(x_1,\ldots,x_n\), the Jacobian is an \(m\times n\) matrix with
real scalar entries

\[
J_{ij}(x_1,\ldots,x_n)
=\frac{\partial}{\partial x_j}\left(F_i(x_1,\ldots,x_n)\right).
\]

The positive integers \(m,n\) specify the output and input dimensions.
All coordinates \(x_k\) with \(k\ne j\) are held fixed. Index \(i\) selects an
output, with \(1\leq i\leq m\); index \(j\) selects an input, with
\(1\leq j\leq n\). Ellipses denote the general coordinate list; actual
coordinates are enumerated in concrete examples.

For a differentiable real scalar function \(f(x,y)\) in ordinary Euclidean
coordinates, the gradient is the column vector

\[
\nabla f(x,y)=
\begin{bmatrix}
\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\left(f(x,y)\right)\\
\frac{\partial}{\partial y}\left(f(x,y)\right)
\end{bmatrix}.
\]

The other coordinate is held fixed in each entry. The scalar function's
Jacobian is the transpose of this gradient and is a one-row matrix. These
objects are distinguished by their defined shapes rather than denoted by
an ambiguous fraction with respect to a vector. The superscript
\(\mathsf T\) denotes transpose.

\subsection{Matrix and tensor inputs}

Derivatives with respect to matrix inputs are described first in terms of
individual scalar entries. For a real scalar function \(\phi(\mathbf A)\) of
a real \(m\times n\) matrix with independently variable entries, the matrix
gradient \(\nabla_{\mathbf A}\phi(\mathbf A)\) has the same shape as
\(\mathbf A\). Its \((i,j)\) entry is the scalar partial derivative with
respect to \(A_{ij}\), with all other entries held fixed. For a symmetric or
otherwise constrained matrix, the independent parameters are identified
separately; all entries cannot then be regarded as independent inputs.

For a differentiable matrix-valued function \(\mathbf F(\mathbf A)\), the
notation \(\mathrm D\mathbf F(\mathbf A)(\mathbf H)\) denotes the derivative
at the matrix input \(\mathbf A\), applied to the increment matrix
\(\mathbf H\). The shapes of both matrices and of the output are specified
with the definition. This expression represents a linear map applied to
an increment rather than matrix division. Parentheses around the
increment argument are used in place of the square brackets found in
some references.

For tensor inputs and outputs, the tensor space, basis, component indices,
and index ranges are specified. In geometric tensor notation, upper and
lower indices have distinct meanings, which are defined with the objects.
Summations are written explicitly. Component differentiation is initially
represented in a fixed basis; variation of the basis introduces additional
terms.

\section{4. Integrals}

For a real scalar-valued integrand \(f(x)\) and fixed real scalar endpoints
\(a,b\), definite integration with respect to the real scalar variable \(x\)
is represented by

\[
\int_a^b f(x)\,dx.
\]

The differential \(dx\) identifies the integration variable. The lower
limit is \(x=a\) and the upper limit is \(x=b\). These endpoint statements
accompany the integral; the bounds themselves contain \(a\) and \(b\).
Parentheses delimit a sum or other compound integrand where necessary.

Within a definite integral, \(x\) is a bound integration variable. When
both limits and all parameters are fixed, the result is a real scalar
number rather than a function of \(x\).

For an indefinite integral on an interval where an antiderivative exists,

\[
\int f(x)\,dx=F(x)+C,
\qquad \frac{d}{dx}\left(F(x)\right)=f(x).
\]

The real scalar constant \(C\) is independent of \(x\). For vector and matrix
outputs, the arbitrary constant has the corresponding output shape.

For a real scalar function with another input \(y\) held fixed,

\[
\int f(x,y)\,dx=F(x,y)+C(y),\qquad y\text{ held fixed}.
\]

The integration constant may depend on the input over which integration
was not performed. Definite integration over \(x\) may likewise leave a
function of \(y\).

Endpoint evaluation of an antiderivative is denoted by

\[
\left.F(x)\right|_{x=a}^{x=b}=F(b)-F(a).
\]

The upper-endpoint value is followed by subtraction of the lower-endpoint
value. Multiple integrals are accompanied by a stated order of integration,
with bounds and a differential for each integration variable. Curve,
surface, and volume integrals have specified domains, measures,
parameterizations where applicable, and orientations where relevant.
Arc-length measure \(ds\), surface-area measure \(dS\), and vector displacement
are distinct objects and are defined separately.

\section{5. Change-of-variable notation}

In a scalar change of variable, the new coordinate is written as
\(u=g(x)\), where \(g(x)\) is a real scalar function of the original real
scalar variable \(x\). Before substitution, \(u\) depends on \(x\); within the
transformed integral, \(u\) is the integration variable. The expression
\(h(u)\) denotes a real scalar function at that new input.

For a continuously differentiable function \(g(x)\) on the interval between
fixed real scalar endpoints \(a,b\), and a continuous function \(h(u)\) on an
interval containing its image, the change of variable is represented by

\[
\int_a^b h(g(x))\frac{d}{dx}\left(g(x)\right)\,dx
=\int_{g(a)}^{g(b)}h(u)\,du.
\]

On the left, the variable is \(x\) and the endpoints are \(a,b\). On the
right, the variable is \(u\) and the endpoints are \(g(a),g(b)\). An inverse
of \(g(x)\) is not required by this form of the substitution theorem. A
formulation involving an inverse may require domain restrictions and a
specified branch.

The substitution, its derivative, the matching factors, and both
transformed endpoints are displayed in worked definite-integral
calculations. In indefinite-integral results, the original input is
restored and the antiderivative relation is verified by differentiation.

The differential identity \(du=2x\,dx\) corresponds to the real scalar
relation \(u=g(x)=x^2+1\). The differential of \(u\) is the derivative of
\(g(x)\) multiplied by the differential of \(x\). This notation expresses a
differential relation; cancellation of the symbols \(d\) and \(x\) is not
its justification. Division by a derivative is valid only where that
derivative is nonzero.

\section{6. Related symbols and distinctions}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Finite change and differential.} \(\Delta x\) denotes a finite real scalar
\end{itemize}
  increment. A differential represents the linear part of a change and
  need not equal the exact finite change. The derivative-map expression
  \(\mathrm Df(x,y)(\Delta x,\Delta y)\) is accompanied by its defining
  partial-derivative formula.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Function inverse and reciprocal.} \(1/f(x)\) denotes a reciprocal where
\end{itemize}
  \(f(x)\ne0\). The expression \(f^{-1}(y)\) denotes a defined inverse function.
  The inverse sine is written as \(\arcsin(x)\).
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Function powers.} The square of a sine value is written as
\end{itemize}
  \((\sin(x))^2\). The function's input and the power applied to its value
  are thereby displayed separately.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Matrix inverse and reciprocal.} \(\mathbf A^{-1}\) denotes a matrix
\end{itemize}
  inverse when it exists. Entrywise reciprocals are defined separately;
  a matrix quotient is not part of the adopted notation.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Absolute value, determinant, and norm.} \(|s|\) denotes the absolute
\end{itemize}
  value of a scalar \(s\); \(\det(\mathbf A)\) denotes the determinant of a
  square matrix; and \(\lVert\mathbf v\rVert\) denotes a specified norm of
  a vector. The chosen norm is defined with its use.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Products.} Scalar multiplication, dot products, cross products,
\end{itemize}
  matrix products, tensor products, and contractions are identified
  when introduced. The order of matrix factors is retained. Entrywise
  products are explicitly identified.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Summations.} Summation signs and their limits are displayed, with
\end{itemize}
  stated ranges for free indices. Individual terms are expanded in
  initial low-dimensional examples. Implicit Einstein summation is not
  used in worked calculations.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Tensors and arrays.} Multilinear tensors and multidimensional data
\end{itemize}
  arrays are related through specified representations; they are not
  treated as identical objects without a definition of that relation.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Equality and approximation.} The symbol \(=\) denotes exact equality;
\end{itemize}
  \(\approx\) denotes an approximation. An approximation is accompanied
  by its validity conditions and an error estimate, or by a statement
  that no error bound has been established.

\subsection{Remainders and limiting behavior}

The symbols \(O\) and \(o\) describe the size of an error in a stated limit. For real
scalar functions \(r(x),g(x)\), with \(g(x)\ne0\) near the limiting point \(a\) except
possibly at \(a\) itself, the statement \(r(x)=O(g(x))\) as \(x\to a\) means that
\(|r(x)|\le M|g(x)|\) near \(a\) for some fixed finite constant \(M\). The statement
\(r(x)=o(g(x))\) means that \(r(x)/g(x)\to0\). In particular, \(o(1)\) denotes a quantity
that tends to zero. The domain determines whether a limit is two-sided or
one-sided, and the direction is stated when it matters.

An estimate such as \(r(x)=O(x^2)\) controls values of the remainder; it does not,
by itself, justify differentiating that estimate. Bounds on derivatives require
additional information. When an actual numerical error is needed, an explicit
constant and interval are given rather than an order symbol alone.

\chapter{Calculus methods reference}

A difficult calculation rarely announces which method will make it manageable.
This reference brings together the formulas and conditions most often needed in
the worked problems. Each entry describes the mathematical structure that makes
a method useful and the restrictions that remain after it has been applied.
The examples in the following chapters combine these methods in different ways.

Unless stated otherwise, the quantities in this reference are real scalars.
Parameters described as fixed remain constant during the displayed operation.
The distinction matters when a later step differentiates an answer with respect
to one of those parameters.

\section{Differentiation and local representations}

\subsection{Elementary derivatives and primitives}

The independent input \(x\) and the fixed parameter \(p\) are real scalars. The
following formulas apply on the indicated real domains. Each primitive is
understood on a connected interval of that domain; \(C\) is an arbitrary constant
on that interval.

\begin{longtable}{@{}>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{0.225\textwidth}>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{0.225\textwidth}>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{0.225\textwidth}>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{0.225\textwidth}@{}}
Scalar expression & Derivative with respect to \(x\) & Primitive with respect to \(x\) & Real-domain restriction \\[5pt]
\hline\endhead
\(x^p\) & \(\frac{d}{dx}(x^p)=px^{p-1}\) & \(\int x^p\,dx=\frac{x^{p+1}}{p+1}+C\), \(p\ne-1\) & \(x>0\) suffices for arbitrary real \(p\); integer powers may have larger domains \\[5pt]
\(1/x\) & \(\frac{d}{dx}(1/x)=-1/x^2\) & \(\int (1/x)\,dx=\ln|x|+C\) & \(x\ne0\) \\[5pt]
\(\exp(x)\) & \(\frac{d}{dx}(\exp(x))=\exp(x)\) & \(\int\exp(x)\,dx=\exp(x)+C\) & All real \(x\) \\[5pt]
\(\ln(x)\) & \(\frac{d}{dx}(\ln(x))=1/x\) & \(\int\ln(x)\,dx=x\ln(x)-x+C\) & \(x>0\) \\[5pt]
\(\sin(x)\) & \(\frac{d}{dx}(\sin(x))=\cos(x)\) & \(\int\sin(x)\,dx=-\cos(x)+C\) & All real \(x\) \\[5pt]
\(\cos(x)\) & \(\frac{d}{dx}(\cos(x))=-\sin(x)\) & \(\int\cos(x)\,dx=\sin(x)+C\) & All real \(x\) \\[5pt]
\(\tan(x)\) & \(\frac{d}{dx}(\tan(x))=1/\cos^2(x)\) & \(\int\tan(x)\,dx=-\ln|\cos(x)|+C\) & \(\cos(x)\ne0\) \\[5pt]
\(\sinh(x)\) & \(\frac{d}{dx}(\sinh(x))=\cosh(x)\) & \(\int\sinh(x)\,dx=\cosh(x)+C\) & All real \(x\) \\[5pt]
\(\cosh(x)\) & \(\frac{d}{dx}(\cosh(x))=\sinh(x)\) & \(\int\cosh(x)\,dx=\sinh(x)+C\) & All real \(x\) \\[5pt]
\(\tanh(x)\) & \(\frac{d}{dx}(\tanh(x))=1/\cosh^2(x)\) & \(\int\tanh(x)\,dx=\ln(\cosh(x))+C\) & All real \(x\) \\[5pt]
\end{longtable}

Inverse-function derivatives often identify a primitive more efficiently than
algebraic expansion. With the standard real branches,

\[
\begin{aligned}
\frac{d}{dx}(\arcsin(x))&=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-x^2}},&&-1<x<1,\\
\frac{d}{dx}(\arccos(x))&=-\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-x^2}},&&-1<x<1,\\
\frac{d}{dx}(\arctan(x))&=\frac{1}{1+x^2},&&x\in\mathbb R,\\
\frac{d}{dx}(\operatorname{arsinh}(x))&=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+x^2}},&&x\in\mathbb R,\\
\frac{d}{dx}(\operatorname{arcosh}(x))&=\frac{1}{\sqrt{x^2-1}},&&x>1,\\
\frac{d}{dx}(\operatorname{artanh}(x))&=\frac{1}{1-x^2},&&-1<x<1.
\end{aligned}
\]

The square roots in these real formulas are nonnegative. Endpoint continuity
of an inverse function does not imply a finite endpoint derivative. The
antiderivative of \(1/(1-x^2)\) also has real branches outside \((-1,1)\):
\(\frac12\ln|(1+x)/(1-x)|+C\) applies on each interval excluding \(x=\pm1\).
Arguments such as \(g(x)\) require the corresponding chain factor or substitution;
the displayed formulas do not remove that dependence.

\subsection{Linearity, products, quotients, and compositions}

For an independent real scalar \(x\), fixed scalars \(a,b\), and differentiable
scalar functions \(u(x),v(x)\),

\[
\begin{aligned}
\frac{d}{dx}\left(au(x)+bv(x)\right)&=a\frac{d}{dx}\left(u(x)\right)+b\frac{d}{dx}\left(v(x)\right),\\
\frac{d}{dx}\left(u(x)v(x)\right)&=\frac{d}{dx}\left(u(x)\right)v(x)+u(x)\frac{d}{dx}\left(v(x)\right),\\
\frac{d}{dx}\left(\frac{u(x)}{v(x)}\right)&=\frac{\frac{d}{dx}(u(x))v(x)-u(x)\frac{d}{dx}(v(x))}{v(x)^2},\quad v(x)\ne0.
\end{aligned}
\]

For \(f(x)=h(g(x))\), with independent real scalar argument \(t\) of the outer
function \(h(t)\), the chain rule is

\[
\frac{d}{dx}\left(f(x)\right)
=\left.\frac{d}{dt}\left(h(t)\right)\right|_{t=g(x)}\frac{d}{dx}\left(g(x)\right).
\]

The formula applies when the inner value lies in a domain where the outer function is differentiable.
Factoring, polynomial division, or a different representation may reduce the
number of rule applications. Cancellation preserves exclusions from the original
domain. Several nested arguments may be represented by separately named functions.

\subsection{Logarithmic differentiation and variable powers}

For differentiable real scalar \(f(x)>0\),

\[
\frac{d}{dx}\left(\ln(f(x))\right)=\frac{\frac{d}{dx}(f(x))}{f(x)}.
\]

A positive base \(a(x)\) with real exponent \(b(x)\) gives
\(f(x)=a(x)^{b(x)}=\exp(b(x)\ln(a(x)))\), hence

\[
\frac{d}{dx}\left(f(x)\right)
=f(x)\left(\frac{d}{dx}(b(x))\ln(a(x))
+b(x)\frac{\frac{d}{dx}(a(x))}{a(x)}\right).
\]

This representation is useful when both base and exponent depend on the input,
or when products and powers would otherwise generate many terms. For nonzero
real \(f(x)\), \(\ln|f(x)|\) has the same logarithmic derivative on each connected
component. Complex logarithms require a selected local branch.

\subsection{Implicit differentiation and coordinate charts}

For a continuously differentiable scalar level function \(F(x,y,z)\) and a local
scalar height \(z=g(x,y)\) satisfying \(F(x,y,g(x,y))=0\),

\[
\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\left(g(x,y)\right)
=-\left.\frac{\frac{\partial}{\partial x}(F(x,y,z))}{\frac{\partial}{\partial z}(F(x,y,z))}\right|_{z=g(x,y)},
\qquad \frac{\partial}{\partial z}\left(F(x,y,z)\right)\ne0.
\]

The independent coordinate \(y\) is held fixed. A vanishing denominator can mean
that the selected height chart fails while another chart remains regular.
The full gradient, rather than one coordinate partial derivative, tests regularity
of the level surface. The same principle applies to implicit scalar relations
\(F(x,y)=0\) and locally defined \(y=g(x)\).

\subsection{Paths, partial derivatives, and second derivatives}

For a scalar field \(f(x,y)\) and a differentiable real path with components
\(x(t),y(t)\), the dependent scalar \(p(t)=f(x(t),y(t))\) satisfies

\[
\frac{d}{dt}\left(p(t)\right)
=\left.\frac{\partial}{\partial x}(f(x,y))\right|_{x=x(t),y=y(t)}\frac{d}{dt}(x(t))
+\left.\frac{\partial}{\partial y}(f(x,y))\right|_{x=x(t),y=y(t)}\frac{d}{dt}(y(t)).
\]

A partial derivative and a derivative along a constrained path are different
operations. Second derivatives differentiate the complete first-derivative
expression, including every dependent factor. Interchanging mixed partials
requires appropriate regularity, such as continuous second partial derivatives
on a neighborhood of the point.

\subsection{Inverse functions, phase, and endpoint limits}

For a differentiable one-to-one scalar function \(g(x)\) with nonzero derivative,
its local inverse \(h(u)\) satisfies

\[
\frac{d}{du}\left(h(u)\right)
=\frac{1}{\left.\frac{d}{dx}(g(x))\right|_{x=h(u)}}.
\]

An inverse branch may require an interval restriction. A finite symbolic
expression does not establish differentiability across a phase jump, cusp,
or excluded point. At a domain endpoint, a one-sided difference quotient is
examined separately. A continuous extension can exist without a finite derivative.
Complex scalar outputs differentiated with respect to real parameters are
handled componentwise; this does not assume complex analyticity.

\section{Antiderivatives and finite integrals}

\subsection{Direct substitution and inverse-coordinate substitution}

For a continuously differentiable real scalar \(g(x)\) and continuous scalar
\(h(u)\) on the required image interval,

\[
\int_a^b h(g(x))\frac{d}{dx}\left(g(x)\right)\,dx
=\int_{g(a)}^{g(b)}h(u)\,du.
\]

The direct chain-rule form does not require \(g\) to be one-to-one. An inverse
coordinate formula instead requires a valid inverse branch \(x=\xi(u)\) and the
factor \(\frac{d}{du}(\xi(u))\). A map with turning points may require partition
into branches. Limits, orientation, integrand, and measure are transformed together.
An unmatched derivative factor cannot be discarded.

\subsection{Integration by parts and recurrences}

For continuously differentiable scalar functions \(u(x),v(x)\) on a finite interval,

\[
\int_a^b u(x)\frac{d}{dx}\left(v(x)\right)\,dx
=u(b)v(b)-u(a)v(a)-\int_a^b\frac{d}{dx}\left(u(x)\right)v(x)\,dx.
\]

This is useful when one factor simplifies under differentiation and another has
an accessible primitive. Repeated applications can produce a recurrence for a
family of integrals or an equation involving the original integral. On unbounded
intervals, every boundary term and remainder integral requires its own limit.
Decay of the integrand alone does not establish that a boundary term vanishes.

\subsection{Rational functions and partial fractions}

Polynomial division separates a rational scalar function into a polynomial and
a proper rational remainder. Factorization over the real numbers then permits
terms associated with real linear factors and irreducible quadratic factors,
including their repeated powers. Coefficients are determined by polynomial
identity or evaluation at suitable inputs. The decomposition retains every
excluded denominator zero. Logarithmic primitives carry absolute values on
real connected components; irreducible quadratics commonly lead to arctangents.

\subsection{Trigonometric and hyperbolic coordinates}

Power reduction, angle addition, and conversion to exponentials can expose a
substitution or an integration-by-parts pattern. The tangent half-angle coordinate
\(u=\tan(x/2)\) gives

\[
\sin x=\frac{2u}{1+u^2},\qquad \cos x=\frac{1-u^2}{1+u^2},
\qquad \frac{d}{du}\left(2\arctan u\right)=\frac{2}{1+u^2}
\]

on a chosen inverse chart. Angular poles and interval orientation remain relevant.
For fixed real \(a>0\), coordinates \(x=a\sin u\), \(x=a\tan u\), or \(x=a\sinh u\)
can resolve square roots of \(a^2-x^2\), \(a^2+x^2\), or related quadratic expressions.
The identity \(\sqrt{q^2}=|q|\) for real \(q\) determines the sign of the transformed
root. Hyperbolic identities and their real inverse branches appear in
T-007.

\subsection{Symmetry, partition, and periodicity}

On a symmetric finite interval, an integrable odd scalar function has zero
integral; an even integrand permits reduction to half the interval. Absolute
values and piecewise definitions are partitioned at sign changes. Periodicity
can reduce a long finite interval to complete periods and a remainder. For an
improper integral, existence is established before symmetric cancellation is
used. A principal-value prescription is a separately named operation.

\subsection{Fundamental theorem and variable limits}

For a continuous scalar \(q(x)\) and a fixed real base point \(a\),
\(F(t)=\int_a^t q(x)\,dx\) has derivative \(q(t)\) at interior inputs. With
differentiable real scalar endpoints \(a(t),b(t)\) and a scalar integrand \(q(x,t)\),

\[
\begin{aligned}
\frac{d}{dt}\left(\int_{a(t)}^{b(t)}q(x,t)\,dx\right)
={}&q(b(t),t)\frac{d}{dt}(b(t))
-q(a(t),t)\frac{d}{dt}(a(t))\\
&+\int_{a(t)}^{b(t)}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left(q(x,t)\right)\,dx.
\end{aligned}
\]

Continuity of \(q\) and its parameter partial derivative on a neighborhood of the
finite integration region is a sufficient local condition. The lower-endpoint
term has a minus sign even when the bounds later reverse their order.

\section{Parameters, convergence, and representations}

\subsection{Differentiation with respect to a parameter}

For a fixed domain \(D\) and scalar \(q(x,t)\), a bound on
\(|\frac{\partial}{\partial t}(q(x,t))|\) that is integrable over \(D\) and valid
uniformly in a neighborhood of the parameter value permits differentiation
under the integral, provided the integral exists at one parameter value and
the integrand has the required measurability and pointwise differentiability.
The new parameter derivative may have a simpler primitive than the original
integrand. Integration back in the parameter introduces a constant independent
of that parameter, which is fixed from a known value or limit. The required
bound may hold near every interior parameter value and fail at its endpoint.

\subsection{Improper integrals, comparison, and principal values}

Endpoint and interior singularities are treated by independent one-sided limits.
For a nonnegative integrand locally comparable to \(x^{-p}\) near zero,
integrability holds for \(p<1\); at infinity it holds for \(p>1\). In radial area or
volume integrals the Jacobian changes this power count. Signed and oscillatory
integrals may converge conditionally. A Cauchy principal value specifies coupled
exclusions; it need not equal an ordinary improper integral or exist under a
different exclusion prescription.

\subsection{Oscillatory integration and damping}

Integration by parts against a bounded oscillatory primitive gives tail bounds
when the amplitude decreases sufficiently. Positive exponential damping can
make an integral absolutely convergent and simplify parameter differentiation.
Removing damping requires a separate limit argument, such as convergence on
finite intervals together with a uniform tail estimate. Absolute domination
cannot be assumed for a conditionally convergent limit. Stationary phase and
steepest descent are asymptotic methods with additional smoothness, contour,
and stationary-point hypotheses; they do not provide exact finite-parameter
identities.

\subsection{Interchanging limits, sums, and integrals}

Dominated convergence requires one integrable dominating function on the domain
under consideration. Monotone convergence applies to increasing nonnegative
measurable sequences. Tonelli's theorem permits rearrangement for nonnegative
integrands; Fubini's theorem provides rearrangement under absolute integrability.
Uniform convergence on a finite interval is a useful sufficient condition for
termwise integration of a function series. Differentiation of a series requires
additional control of the derivative series. Pointwise convergence alone is insufficient: narrow peaks or slowly decaying tails
can contribute to an integral even when they disappear at each fixed input.

\subsection{Series, asymptotics, and special functions}

Taylor polynomials retain a remainder estimate; infinite series retain their
convergence domain. An asymptotic expansion orders terms in a specified limit
and need not converge when continued indefinitely. Special functions can provide
an exact representation of a non-elementary primitive or definite integral.
A remaining numerical integral is accompanied by a specified domain, convergence
analysis, and a usable error estimate. Definitions and branch conventions are
stated locally when a special function first appears. Supporting expansions are
collected in T-002.

\section{Fields, geometry, and multilinear objects}

\subsection{Gradients, Jacobians, and constrained variations}

A scalar field's Euclidean gradient is a column vector of coordinate partial
derivatives. A vector-valued map's Jacobian is the matrix of its component partials,
with output index first and input index second. Along a path, multiplication of
the Jacobian by the path tangent gives the vector derivative. A Hessian is the
matrix of second scalar partials. A constrained directional derivative requires
a tangent direction satisfying the constraint, rather than an arbitrary coordinate
variation. Lagrange multipliers express regular constrained stationary conditions;
constraint regularity and boundary cases are examined independently.

\subsection{Area, volume, and surface measures}

For a one-to-one regular coordinate map in two or three dimensions, integration
uses the absolute determinant of the coordinate Jacobian. For an independent
real surface pair \(u,v\) and real vector map \(\mathbf r(u,v)\), the area factor is

\[
\left\|\frac{\partial}{\partial u}\left(\mathbf r(u,v)\right)
\times\frac{\partial}{\partial v}\left(\mathbf r(u,v)\right)\right\|.
\]

The oriented cross product, rather than its norm, supplies a flux measure.
Line integrals use the path tangent for vector work and its norm for scalar
arc-length integration. Coordinate degeneracies at seams or poles may occupy
sets of measure zero; many-to-one maps require partition or multiplicity accounting.

\subsection{Integral theorems and excluded sources}

Green's theorem, Stokes' theorem, and the divergence theorem replace suitable
integrals by boundary or interior integrals with consistent orientation and
regularity. For a continuously differentiable real vector field \(\mathbf F(x,y,z)\)
on a neighborhood of a bounded volume \(V\) with piecewise smooth boundary,

\[
\iint_{\partial V}\mathbf F(x,y,z)\cdot\mathbf n(x,y,z)\,dS
=\iiint_V\nabla\cdot\mathbf F(x,y,z)\,dx\,dy\,dz.
\]

The real vector \(\mathbf n(x,y,z)\) is the outward unit normal. A source singularity
inside \(V\) violates the classical regularity hypothesis. Excision introduces an
additional inner boundary, with its own orientation and flux. Boundary
conditions and topology can therefore determine an answer before a lengthy
coordinate integration is attempted.

\subsection{Matrix systems and tensor components}

For a differentiable invertible square matrix \(\mathbf A(t)\) and fixed column
vector \(\mathbf b\), the dependent vector \(\mathbf j(t)=\mathbf A(t)^{-1}\mathbf b\)
satisfies

\[
\frac{d}{dt}\left(\mathbf j(t)\right)
=-\mathbf A(t)^{-1}\frac{d}{dt}\left(\mathbf A(t)\right)\mathbf j(t).
\]

Matrix order is retained. Matrix and tensor derivatives can be computed
componentwise after the spaces, shapes, index ranges, and contractions are
specified. For a dependent order-three tensor with scalar components
\(T_{ijk}(t)\), its derivative components are \(\frac{d}{dt}(T_{ijk}(t))\); its
integral components are \(\int_a^bT_{ijk}(t)\,dt\) on a stated real interval.
A tensor representation in a varying basis additionally includes basis derivatives.
Near a singular matrix, sensitivity depends on the right-hand side and cannot
be inferred from a canceled component formula alone.

\section{D4-001 --- A variable power near an excluded input}
\label{problem:D4-001}
\markright{D4-001}
{\small Hints: page~\pageref{hints:D4-001}.}\par\medskip

\textbf{Objects and dependencies.} \(x\) is an independent real scalar, \(x\ne0\). The real scalar function is

\[
f(x)=\bigl(\ln(1+x^2)\bigr)^{\arctan x}.
\]

Determine its derivative on its domain and whether it has a continuous, differentiable extension at \(x=0\). The real power is defined through the logarithm of its positive base.
\subsection*{Worked solution}

\textbf{Step 1 --- SETUP.} The real base \(a(x)=\ln(1+x^2)\) is positive for \(x\ne0\). The real exponent is \(b(x)=\arctan x\), and the output \(f(x)>0\) is differentiable on each component of the punctured real line.

\begin{ChoiceStep}
\textbf{Step 2 --- CHOICE.} Both the base and the exponent vary with \(x\), so differentiating the power involves two contributions. Taking a logarithm turns the power into the product \(b(x)\ln(a(x))\). Equivalently, \(f(x)=\exp(b(x)\ln(a(x)))\); either representation keeps both contributions visible.

\end{ChoiceStep}
\textbf{Step 3 --- CALCULUS.} The two inner derivatives are

\[
\frac{d}{dx}(a(x))=\frac{2x}{1+x^2},
\qquad
\frac{d}{dx}(b(x))=\frac{1}{1+x^2}.
\]

Differentiation of \(\ln(f(x))=b(x)\ln(a(x))\) gives

\[
\frac{1}{f(x)}\frac{d}{dx}(f(x))
=\frac{d}{dx}(b(x))\ln(a(x))
+\frac{b(x)}{a(x)}\frac{d}{dx}(a(x)).
\]

Multiplication by \(f(x)\) and substitution of the two derivatives yield

\[
\frac{d}{dx}\left(f(x)\right)
=f(x)\left(\frac{\ln(\ln(1+x^2))}{1+x^2}
+\frac{2x\arctan x}{(1+x^2)\ln(1+x^2)}\right),\qquad x\ne0.
\]

\begingroup\color{TrickPurple}
\textbf{Step 4 --- TRICK: local expansion.} The real Taylor expansions in T-002 give \(\ln(1+x^2)=x^2(1+O(x^2))\) and \(\arctan x=x+O(x^3)\) as \(x\to0\). Consequently,

\[
\ln(f(x))=2x\ln|x|+O(x^3|\ln|x||)+O(x^3)\longrightarrow0.
\]

\endgroup
\begin{DomainStep}
\textbf{Step 5 --- DOMAIN.} The unique continuous extension has value \(\widetilde f(0)=1\). Since \(\exp(s)-1=s+O(s^2)\) near \(s=0\), its difference quotient satisfies

\[
\frac{\widetilde f(x)-\widetilde f(0)}{x}
=2\ln|x|+o(1)\longrightarrow-\infty.
\]

The error from the exponential, divided by \(x\), is \(O(|x|\ln^2|x|)\to0\). Thus the extension has no finite derivative at zero.

\end{DomainStep}
\textbf{Step 6 --- CHECK.} For \(x\ne0\), the original derivative formula divided by \(f(x)\) is the derivative of \(\ln(f(x))\). Its second term tends to two, while the first tends to \(-\infty\), consistent with the endpoint analysis. A finite function limit does not supply differentiability.

\textbf{Discussion.} Variable powers require both derivatives and an explicit positive-base domain. Extension of the value and extension of a finite derivative are separate questions.

\section{D4-002 --- An angle defined by a hyperbolic function}
\label{problem:D4-002}
\markright{D4-002}
{\small Hints: page~\pageref{hints:D4-002}.}\par\medskip

\textbf{Objects and dependencies.} \(x\in\mathbb R\) is an independent dimensionless real scalar. The dependent real angle is \(\theta(x)=\arccos(\tanh x)\) in \((0,\pi)\). Determine its first and second derivatives, with a representation valid for every finite real \(x\).
\subsection*{Worked solution}

\textbf{Step 1 --- SETUP.} At every finite real input, \(-1<\tanh x<1\), and \(0<\theta(x)<\pi\). The inverse cosine branch and its derivatives are therefore defined.

\begin{ChoiceStep}
\textbf{Step 2 --- CHOICE.} Implicit differentiation of the defining cosine relation avoids a nested inverse-function quotient and makes the sign choice visible.

\end{ChoiceStep}
\textbf{Step 3 --- CALCULUS.} Differentiation with respect to \(x\) gives

\[
-\sin(\theta(x))\frac{d}{dx}\left(\theta(x)\right)=\frac{1}{\cosh^2x}.
\]

\begingroup\color{TrickPurple}
\textbf{Step 4 --- TRICK: hyperbolic identities.} By T-007, and the positive sine on the chosen branch,

\[
\sin(\theta(x))=\sqrt{1-\tanh^2x}=\frac{1}{\cosh x}.
\]

\endgroup
\begingroup\color{AlgebraBlue}
\textbf{Step 5 --- ALGEBRA.} Division by the strictly positive sine gives

\[
\frac{d}{dx}\left(\theta(x)\right)=-\frac{1}{\cosh x}.
\]

\endgroup
\textbf{Step 6 --- CALCULUS.} Differentiation of the complete first derivative yields

\[
\frac{d}{dx}\left(\frac{d}{dx}\left(\theta(x)\right)\right)
=\frac{\sinh x}{\cosh^2x}.
\]

\begin{DomainStep}
\textbf{Step 7 --- CHECK AND DOMAIN.} \(\cosh x\ge1\) excludes finite denominator zeros. At \(x=0\), the angle is \(\pi/2\), its first derivative is \(-1\), and its second derivative is zero. The angle decreases from a limit of \(\pi\) to a limit of zero as \(x\) increases; those limiting angles are not attained at finite inputs.

\end{DomainStep}
\textbf{Discussion.} An inverse-function derivative can simplify considerably after the defining relation and branch sign are retained.

\section{D4-003 --- A logarithmic ratio of radicals}
\label{problem:D4-003}
\markright{D4-003}
{\small Hints: page~\pageref{hints:D4-003}.}\par\medskip

\textbf{Objects and dependencies.} \(x\in\mathbb R\) is an independent real scalar and

\[
f(x)=\ln\left(\frac{\sqrt{1+x^2}+x}{\sqrt{1+x^2}-x}\right).
\]

Determine the real domain, the derivative, and an equivalent expression that avoids subtracting nearly equal positive quantities for large positive \(x\).
\subsection*{Worked solution}

\textbf{Step 1 --- SETUP.} The independent variable is real, and the square root is nonnegative. For every finite \(x\), \(\sqrt{1+x^2}>|x|\), so both logarithmic factors are positive and the domain is all of \(\mathbb R\).

\begin{ChoiceStep}
\textbf{Step 2 --- CHOICE.} The conjugate product can remove the denominator before the logarithm is differentiated. This avoids a long quotient-rule calculation and preserves positivity.

\end{ChoiceStep}
\begingroup\color{AlgebraBlue}
\textbf{Step 3 --- ALGEBRA.} The exact identity

\[
(\sqrt{1+x^2}+x)(\sqrt{1+x^2}-x)=1+x^2-x^2=1
\]

implies that the ratio is \((\sqrt{1+x^2}+x)^2\). Thus

\[
f(x)=2\ln(\sqrt{1+x^2}+x).
\]

\endgroup
\textbf{Step 4 --- CALCULUS.} Logarithmic and chain differentiation give

\[
\frac{d}{dx}\left(f(x)\right)
=2\frac{x/\sqrt{1+x^2}+1}{\sqrt{1+x^2}+x}.
\]

\begingroup\color{AlgebraBlue}
\textbf{Step 5 --- ALGEBRA.} The numerator is \((x+\sqrt{1+x^2})/\sqrt{1+x^2}\), so cancellation on the positive domain gives \(\frac{d}{dx}(f(x))=2/\sqrt{1+x^2}\).

\endgroup
\begingroup\color{TrickPurple}
\textbf{Step 6 --- TRICK AND CHECK.} The real inverse identity in T-007 gives \(f(x)=2\operatorname{arsinh}x\). For large positive \(x\) this representation avoids the small difference in the original denominator. For negative \(x\), oddness gives \(f(x)=-2\ln(\sqrt{1+x^2}-x)\), avoiding cancellation in the other factor. The reciprocal factors also show \(f(-x)=-f(x)\) directly.

\endgroup
\textbf{Discussion.} Algebraic equivalence can improve both differentiation and numerical evaluation. The stable representation depends on the sign of the input.

\section{D4-004 --- A coupled quadric and a constrained path}
\label{problem:D4-004}
\markright{D4-004}
{\small Hints: page~\pageref{hints:D4-004}.}\par\medskip

\textbf{Objects and dependencies.} The real scalar level function is \(F(x,y,z)=x^2+2xz+2z^2+y^2-1\). On its upper height branch, the dependent scalar \(z=g(x,y)\) satisfies \(F(x,y,g(x,y))=0\) and \(x+2g(x,y)>0\). Determine the height and its partial derivatives on their open domain. Along the real path \(x(t)=\cos t\), \(y(t)=\sin t/\sqrt2\), determine \(\frac{d}{dt}(g(x(t),y(t)))\) and verify it using both a path formula and the explicit height.
\subsection*{Worked solution}

\textbf{Step 1 --- SETUP.} Independent real \(x,y\) determine a dependent real height. The path parameter \(t\) is an independent real angle. The condition \(x+2g(x,y)>0\) selects one height branch.

\begin{ChoiceStep}
\textbf{Step 2 --- CHOICE.} A quadratic solution establishes the graph domain; implicit differentiation organizes the coordinate partials. The derivative along the path then combines both partials, rather than holding either path coordinate fixed.

\end{ChoiceStep}
\begingroup\color{AlgebraBlue}
\textbf{Step 3 --- ALGEBRA.} The quadratic formula gives

\[
g(x,y)=\frac{-x+\sqrt{D(x,y)}}2,\qquad D(x,y)=2-x^2-2y^2>0.
\]

On this branch \(2x+4g(x,y)=2\sqrt{D(x,y)}\).

\endgroup
\textbf{Step 4 --- CALCULUS.} The level partials are \(2x+2z\), \(2y\), and \(2x+4z\). Hence

\[
\frac{\partial}{\partial x}(g(x,y))=-\frac12-\frac{x}{2\sqrt{D(x,y)}},\qquad
\frac{\partial}{\partial y}(g(x,y))=-\frac{y}{\sqrt{D(x,y)}}.
\]

\begingroup\color{AlgebraBlue}
\textbf{Step 5 --- ALGEBRA AND DOMAIN.} On the path, \(D(x(t),y(t))=2-\cos^2t-\sin^2t=1\). Every finite real \(t\) remains strictly inside the graph domain. At \(D=0\) the two height branches meet and the height partials are not supplied by these formulas; the full quadric surface remains regular because its gradient vanishes only at the origin, which is not on \(F=0\).

\endgroup
\textbf{Step 6 --- CALCULUS.} The path derivatives are \(-\sin t\) and \(\cos t/\sqrt2\), so

\[
\begin{aligned}
\frac{d}{dt}\left(g(x(t),y(t))\right)
&=\left(-\frac12-\frac{\cos t}{2}\right)(-\sin t)
-\frac{\sin t}{\sqrt2}\frac{\cos t}{\sqrt2}\\
&=\frac{\sin t}{2}+\frac{\cos t\sin t}{2}-\frac{\sin t\cos t}{2}
=\frac{\sin t}{2}.
\end{aligned}
\]

\textbf{Step 7 --- CHECK.} Direct substitution gives \(g(x(t),y(t))=(1-\cos t)/2\), whose derivative agrees. Cancellation of the mixed path contributions is a consequence of this specific constraint, not permission to omit either coordinate derivative.

\textbf{Discussion.} A complicated height formula may simplify on a constrained path. The graph domain and the path dependence are assessed separately.

\section{D4-005 --- A complex response with a delay parameter}
\label{problem:D4-005}
\markright{D4-005}
{\small Hints: page~\pageref{hints:D4-005}.}\par\medskip

\textbf{Objects and dependencies.} \(\omega\in\mathbb R\) is an independent real angular frequency. Fixed real times \(\tau>0\) and \(T\ge0\) define the complex scalar response

\[
H(\omega;\tau,T)=\frac{1+\mathrm{i}\omega\tau}{1-\mathrm{i}\omega\tau}\exp(-\mathrm{i}\omega T),\qquad \mathrm{i}^2=-1.
\]

Determine a continuous real phase with value zero at \(\omega=0\), its frequency derivative, and the scalar quantity \(-\frac{\partial}{\partial\omega}(\phi(\omega;\tau,T))\). State when the latter is negative and what happens if the principal phase is used instead.
\subsection*{Worked solution}

\textbf{Step 1 --- SETUP.} The parameters \(\tau,T\) are fixed, and the response is nonzero for every real \(\omega\). Its magnitude is one because \(|1+\mathrm{i}\omega\tau|=|1-\mathrm{i}\omega\tau|\).

\begin{ChoiceStep}
\textbf{Step 2 --- CHOICE.} A continuous phase can be assembled from the two factors whose real parts equal one, avoiding principal-argument jumps during differentiation.

\end{ChoiceStep}
\textbf{Step 3 --- CALCULUS.} The required real phase and its derivative are

\[
\phi(\omega;\tau,T)=2\arctan(\omega\tau)-\omega T,\qquad
\frac{\partial}{\partial\omega}\left(\phi(\omega;\tau,T)\right)
=\frac{2\tau}{1+\omega^2\tau^2}-T.
\]

\begingroup\color{AlgebraBlue}
\textbf{Step 4 --- ALGEBRA.} The negative derivative is \(T-2\tau/(1+\omega^2\tau^2)\). For \(T=0\) it is negative for every finite frequency. For \(0<T<2\tau\) it is negative precisely when

\[
|\omega|<\frac{\sqrt{2\tau/T-1}}{\tau}.
\]

For \(T\ge2\tau\) it is nonnegative everywhere, with equality at zero when \(T=2\tau\).

\endgroup
\begin{DomainStep}
\textbf{Step 5 --- DOMAIN.} A principal phase agrees locally with this phase plus an integer multiple of \(2\pi\). Its derivative agrees on each smooth chart, but it is undefined at any branch jump. No causality or passivity assumption has been imposed on this mathematical frequency response; such physical conditions cannot be inferred from its unit magnitude.

\end{DomainStep}
\textbf{Step 6 --- CHECK.} The quotient rule, followed by division by the nonzero response, gives

\[
\frac{1}{H(\omega;\tau,T)}
\frac{\partial}{\partial\omega}\left(H(\omega;\tau,T)\right)
=\mathrm{i}\left(\frac{2\tau}{1+\omega^2\tau^2}-T\right).
\]

This logarithmic derivative is purely imaginary, consistent with the constant magnitude. The negative phase derivative has units of time.

\textbf{Discussion.} A continuous phase representation separates parameter sensitivity from branch artifacts. A mathematical response formula alone does not impose additional physical constraints.

\section{D4-006 --- An integral with two moving endpoints}
\label{problem:D4-006}
\markright{D4-006}
{\small Hints: page~\pageref{hints:D4-006}.}\par\medskip

\textbf{Objects and dependencies.} \(t\in\mathbb R\) is an independent dimensionless real scalar. The real integration variable is \(s\), and the real scalar function is

\[
F(t)=\int_{t^2}^{\exp t}\frac{\exp(-ts^2)}{1+s^2}\,ds.
\]

Determine its derivative for finite real \(t\) and evaluate it at zero. The integral is oriented if the endpoints reverse order. A general elementary closed form for \(F(t)\) is not required.
\subsection*{Worked solution}

\textbf{Step 1 --- SETUP.} Both endpoints are finite nonnegative real scalars for finite \(t\). The kernel \(q(s,t)=\exp(-ts^2)/(1+s^2)\) is real and smooth for all real \(s,t\).

\begin{ChoiceStep}
\textbf{Step 2 --- CHOICE.} A primitive of the parameter-dependent kernel is unnecessary. The variable-limit rule accounts separately for its parameter dependence and both endpoint velocities.

\end{ChoiceStep}
\textbf{Step 3 --- CALCULUS.} With \(s\) fixed, \(\frac{\partial}{\partial t}(q(s,t))=-s^2\exp(-ts^2)/(1+s^2)\). The endpoint derivatives are \(2t\) and \(\exp t\). Consequently,

\[
\begin{aligned}
\frac{d}{dt}\left(F(t)\right)
={}&\frac{\exp t\,\exp(-t\exp(2t))}{1+\exp(2t)}
-\frac{2t\exp(-t^5)}{1+t^4}\\
&-\int_{t^2}^{\exp t}\frac{s^2\exp(-ts^2)}{1+s^2}\,ds.
\end{aligned}
\]

\begin{DomainStep}
\textbf{Step 4 --- DOMAIN.} For \(t\) in a sufficiently small closed interval around any finite value, every integration interval lies inside one bounded interval of \(s\). The resulting rectangle in \((s,t)\) is compact. The kernel and its parameter derivative are continuous there and hence bounded. The local differentiation theorem therefore applies for every real \(t\), including values at which the endpoints coincide or reverse order.

\end{DomainStep}
\begingroup\color{AlgebraBlue}
\textbf{Step 5 --- ALGEBRA.} At \(t=0\) the boundary terms reduce to \(1/2\) and zero. The remaining integral is

\[
\int_0^1\frac{s^2}{1+s^2}\,ds
=\int_0^1\left(1-\frac{1}{1+s^2}\right)\,ds=1-\frac\pi4.
\]

\endgroup
\textbf{Step 6 --- RESULT AND CHECK.} The requested derivative is \(\frac{d}{dt}(F(t))|_{t=0}=\pi/4-1/2\). Also \(F(0)=\pi/4\). The first boundary contribution is positive at zero, while the interior contribution is negative; both are needed to obtain the stated slope. Independent finite differences of the original integral verify the value.

\textbf{Discussion.} A derivative of an integral can be tractable even when its parameter-dependent antiderivative is not. Endpoint motion and interior dependence produce distinct terms.

\markright{D3-001}
{\small Hints: page~\pageref{hints:D3-001}.}\par\medskip

\textbf{Objects and dependencies.} \(\omega>0\) is an independent real scalar angular frequency. The real scalars \(c>0\) (wave speed) and \(\omega_c>0\) (cutoff frequency) are fixed. Define the nonnegative real scalar propagation constant

\[
\beta(\omega)=\frac{1}{c}\sqrt{\omega^2-\omega_c^2},\qquad \omega\ge\omega_c.
\]

Find \(\frac{d}{d\omega}(\beta(\omega))\) on its differentiability domain, the right-hand behavior at cutoff, and the group velocity \(v_g(\omega)=\frac{1}{\frac{d}{d\omega}\left(\beta(\omega)\right)}\) for \(\omega>\omega_c\). Describe why extending this real formula below cutoff is invalid.
\subsection*{Worked solution}

\textbf{Step 1 --- SETUP.} The variable is the real scalar \(\omega\); \(c\) and \(\omega_c\) are fixed. The output \(\beta(\omega)\) is real on \([\omega_c,\infty)\) and smooth on \((\omega_c,\infty)\). The derivative is sought on that open interval first.

\begin{ChoiceStep}
\textbf{Step 2 --- CHOICE.} The square root is a composition. Its derivative rule requires a strictly positive radicand here; the endpoint therefore needs a separate limit.

\end{ChoiceStep}
\textbf{Step 3 --- CALCULUS.} With respect to \(\omega\),

\[
\frac{d}{d\omega}\left(\beta(\omega)\right)
=\frac{1}{c}\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\omega^2-\omega_c^2}}\frac{d}{d\omega}\left(\omega^2-\omega_c^2\right)
=\frac{\omega}{c\sqrt{\omega^2-\omega_c^2}}.
\]

\begingroup\color{AlgebraBlue}
\textbf{Step 4 --- ALGEBRA.} For \(\omega>\omega_c\) the derivative is positive, so its reciprocal is defined:

\[
v_g(\omega)=\frac{c\sqrt{\omega^2-\omega_c^2}}{\omega}
=c\sqrt{1-\frac{\omega_c^2}{\omega^2}}.
\]

\endgroup
\begin{DomainStep}
\textbf{Step 5 --- DOMAIN.} For a real scalar increment \(h>0\),

\[
\frac{\beta(\omega_c+h)-\beta(\omega_c)}{h}
=\frac{\sqrt{2\omega_c h+h^2}}{ch}
=\frac{\sqrt{2\omega_c+h}}{c\sqrt h}\longrightarrow+\infty.
\]

There is no finite right derivative at cutoff. Below cutoff the radicand is negative. In the convention \(\exp(\mathrm{i}\omega t-\mathrm{i}\beta z)\), a decaying field for \(z>0\) has \(\beta=-\mathrm{i}\alpha\), where \(\alpha=\sqrt{\omega_c^2-\omega^2}/c>0\) is a real attenuation constant. That is a different branch and a different physical regime.

\end{DomainStep}
\textbf{Step 6 --- CHECK.} The derivative has units of inverse velocity. The limits are \(v_g(\omega)\to0\) as \(\omega\downarrow\omega_c\) and \(v_g(\omega)\to c\) as \(\omega\to\infty\). Large sensitivity near cutoff is expected; a high-frequency approximation is unreliable there.

\textbf{Discussion.} A parameter-dependent square root often marks the boundary between regimes. Its endpoint is part of the function's domain without being part of the finite-derivative domain.

\label{problem:D3-002}

\textbf{Objects and dependencies.} \(x,y,z\) are independent real scalar observation coordinates. Fixed real scalars \(x_s,y_s,z_s\) locate a source. The fixed real scalar wavenumber is \(k\ge0\). Define

R(x,y,z)=\sqrt{(x-x_s)^2+(y-y_s)^2+(z-z_s)^2},\qquad
G(x,y,z)=\frac{\exp(-\mathrm{i}kR(x,y,z))}{4\pi R(x,y,z)}.
\]

The real distance satisfies \(R(x,y,z)>0\); \(G(x,y,z)\) is complex scalar-valued. Find the complex column vector \(\nabla_{x,y,z}G(x,y,z)\), with the source and \(k\) held fixed. State its behavior as the source is approached.
\subsection*{Worked solution}

\textbf{Step 1 --- SETUP.} The observation coordinates are independent real scalars, the source and \(k\) are fixed, and \(G(x,y,z)\) is a complex scalar on the punctured space \(R(x,y,z)>0\). The derivative output is a complex \(3\times1\) vector.

\begin{ChoiceStep}
\textbf{Step 2 --- CHOICE.} Dependence through distance separates the radial derivative from the coordinate derivatives. A separate positive real scalar \(\rho\) gives \(g(\rho)=\exp(-\mathrm{i}k\rho)/(4\pi\rho)\) and \(G(x,y,z)=g(R(x,y,z))\).

\end{ChoiceStep}
\textbf{Step 3 --- CALCULUS.} The product rule and chain rule give

\[
\begin{aligned}
\frac{d}{d\rho}\left(g(\rho)\right)
&=\frac{1}{4\pi}\left((-\mathrm{i}k)\exp(-\mathrm{i}k\rho)\rho^{-1}-\exp(-\mathrm{i}k\rho)\rho^{-2}\right),\\
\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\left(R(x,y,z)\right)&=\frac{x-x_s}{R(x,y,z)},\\
\frac{\partial}{\partial y}\left(R(x,y,z)\right)&=\frac{y-y_s}{R(x,y,z)},\\
\frac{\partial}{\partial z}\left(R(x,y,z)\right)&=\frac{z-z_s}{R(x,y,z)}.
\end{aligned}
\]

In each partial derivative the other two coordinates are held fixed.

\begingroup\color{AlgebraBlue}
\textbf{Step 4 --- ALGEBRA.} Factoring the radial derivative gives

\[
\frac{d}{d\rho}\left(g(\rho)\right)
=-\frac{\exp(-\mathrm{i}k\rho)(1+\mathrm{i}k\rho)}{4\pi\rho^2}.
\]

Multiplication by the distance gradient yields

\[
\nabla_{x,y,z}G(x,y,z)
=-\frac{\exp(-\mathrm{i}kR(x,y,z))(1+\mathrm{i}kR(x,y,z))}{4\pi R(x,y,z)^3}
\begin{bmatrix}x-x_s\\y-y_s\\z-z_s\end{bmatrix}.
\]

\endgroup
\textbf{Step 5 --- CHECK.} For \(k=0\), the Coulomb-kernel gradient is recovered. The displacement vector has magnitude \(R(x,y,z)\), so the gradient magnitude behaves as \(1/(4\pi R(x,y,z)^2)\) near the source. The kernel itself behaves as \(1/(4\pi R(x,y,z))\). Neither formula defines a value at the source. A distributional source equation cannot be inferred by simply substituting \(R=0\) into these classical derivatives.

\textbf{Discussion.} A radial chain rule reduces component work, but it preserves the excluded source point. Differentiation strengthens the singularity and can change whether a later integral exists.

\textbf{Step 2 --- CHOICE.} The graph chain rule gives derivatives in its open domain. The level-set gradient gives a normal without selecting an upper or lower height branch.

\end{ChoiceStep}

\[
\begin{aligned}
\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\left(g(x,y)\right)&=-\frac{cx}{a^2\sqrt{1-x^2/a^2-y^2/b^2}},\\

\end{aligned}
\]

For the level function, each of \(x,y,z\) is independent during differentiation:

\[
\nabla_{x,y,z}F(x,y,z)=\begin{bmatrix}2x/a^2\\2y/b^2\\2z/c^2\end{bmatrix}.
\]

\begingroup\color{AlgebraBlue}
\textbf{Step 4 --- ALGEBRA.} Dividing this real vector by its Euclidean length cancels its common factor two:

\[
\mathbf n(x,y,z)=\frac{\begin{bmatrix}x/a^2\\y/b^2\\z/c^2\end{bmatrix}}
{\sqrt{x^2/a^4+y^2/b^4+z^2/c^4}}.
\]

It points outward since \(F\) increases away from the ellipsoid.

\endgroup

\textbf{Step 5 --- DOMAIN AND CHECK.} At the equator \(z=0\), the \(z\) partial derivative of \(F\) vanishes, so the implicit-function theorem does not provide a height graph there. \end{DomainStep}
\textbf{Discussion.} The failure of one coordinate chart is not the failure of the underlying geometry. The denominator in implicit differentiation identifies which chart is available.

\end{DomainStep}

\markright{D3-007}
{\small Hints: page~\pageref{hints:D3-007}.}\par\medskip

\textbf{Objects and dependencies.} \(t\) is an independent dimensionless real scalar. A fixed complex column vector is \(\mathbf b=[b_1,b_2]^{\mathsf T}\). Define the real \(2\times2\) matrix and dependent complex column vector

\[
\mathbf A(t)=\begin{bmatrix}1&t\\t&1\end{bmatrix},\qquad
\mathbf j(t)=\mathbf A(t)^{-1}\mathbf b,\qquad t\ne-1,1.
\]

Find \(\frac{d}{dt}(\mathbf j(t))\) without treating matrix division as scalar division. Also find the component formulas and determine the behavior near \(t=1\) for \(\mathbf b=[1,0]^{\mathsf T}\) and \(\mathbf b=[1,1]^{\mathsf T}\). This small system models a parameter-dependent discretized field equation; it is not a full electromagnetic solver.
\subsection*{Worked solution}

\textbf{Step 1 --- SETUP.} The variable is the real scalar \(t\); \(\mathbf b\) is fixed and complex. \(\mathbf A(t)\) is a real matrix and \(\mathbf j(t)\) a complex \(2\times1\) vector. The matrix is invertible exactly when \(1-t^2\ne0\).

\begin{ChoiceStep}
\textbf{Step 2 --- CHOICE.} Differentiation of the defining linear system preserves multiplication order and avoids a nonexistent rule for scalar-style matrix division.

\end{ChoiceStep}
\textbf{Step 3 --- CALCULUS.} The matrix product rule is applied entry by entry:

\[
\frac{d}{dt}\left(\mathbf A(t)\right)\mathbf j(t)
+\mathbf A(t)\frac{d}{dt}\left(\mathbf j(t)\right)=\mathbf0,
\qquad \frac{d}{dt}\left(\mathbf A(t)\right)=\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{bmatrix}.
\]

Multiplication on the left by the inverse gives

\[
\frac{d}{dt}\left(\mathbf j(t)\right)
=-\mathbf A(t)^{-1}\frac{d}{dt}\left(\mathbf A(t)\right)\mathbf A(t)^{-1}\mathbf b.
\]

\begingroup\color{AlgebraBlue}
\textbf{Step 4 --- ALGEBRA.} The explicit inverse and solution are

\[
\mathbf A(t)^{-1}=\frac{1}{1-t^2}\begin{bmatrix}1&-t\\-t&1\end{bmatrix},\qquad
\mathbf j(t)=\frac{1}{1-t^2}\begin{bmatrix}b_1-tb_2\\b_2-tb_1\end{bmatrix}.
\]

The quotient rule and numerator expansion produce

\[
\frac{d}{dt}\left(\mathbf j(t)\right)
=\frac{1}{(1-t^2)^2}\begin{bmatrix}
-b_2(1-t^2)+2t(b_1-tb_2)\\
-b_1(1-t^2)+2t(b_2-tb_1)
\end{bmatrix}
=\frac{1}{(1-t^2)^2}\begin{bmatrix}2tb_1-(1+t^2)b_2\\2tb_2-(1+t^2)b_1\end{bmatrix}.
\]

\endgroup
\begin{DomainStep}
\textbf{Step 5 --- DOMAIN AND CHECK.} For \(\mathbf b=[1,0]^{\mathsf T}\), the solution diverges at \(t=1\), and the singular system there is inconsistent. For \(\mathbf b=[1,1]^{\mathsf T}\), cancellation on \(t\ne\pm1\) gives \(\mathbf j(t)=[1,1]^{\mathsf T}/(1+t)\) and derivative \(-[1,1]^{\mathsf T}/(1+t)^2\). This solution branch has a finite extension at \(t=1\), but the matrix inverse still does not exist there; the singular system has infinitely many solutions. Substitution into the original nonsingular system verifies both component formulas.

\end{DomainStep}
\textbf{Discussion.} The sensitivity of a matrix solve depends on the right-hand side as well as proximity to a singular matrix. Cancellation in one solution does not extend the inverse operator.

\section{I4-001 --- A difference of exponential kernels}
\label{problem:I4-001}
\markright{I4-001}
{\small Hints: page~\pageref{hints:I4-001}.}\par\medskip

\textbf{Objects and dependencies.} \(x>0\) is a real integration coordinate. Fixed real scalars \(a,b>0\) define the real scalar integral

\[
J(a,b)=\int_0^\infty\frac{\exp(-ax)-\exp(-bx)}{x}\,dx.
\]

Determine its value, convergence at both ends, and behavior as \(a\downarrow0\) with \(b\) fixed. The ratio \(b/a\) is dimensionless.
\subsection*{Worked solution}

\textbf{Step 1 --- SETUP.} The parameters \(a,b\) are positive and fixed, and \(x\) is the integration coordinate. At zero, expansion of the numerator gives \((b-a)x+O(x^2)\), so the kernel has the finite limit \(b-a\). At infinity its absolute value is bounded by \(2\exp(-\min(a,b)x)/x\) beyond \(x=1\).

\begin{ChoiceStep}
\textbf{Step 2 --- CHOICE.} The parameter representation removes the apparent reciprocal singularity before either exponential integral is separated. The two separate integrals would each diverge at zero.

\end{ChoiceStep}
\textbf{Step 3 --- CALCULUS.} With \(x>0\) fixed,

\[
\int_a^b\exp(-sx)\,ds=\frac{\exp(-ax)-\exp(-bx)}{x}.
\]

For \(b\ge a\), the integrand in this representation is nonnegative, so Tonelli's theorem permits interchange. For \(b<a\), the reversed parameter interval changes the sign and the same argument applies to its magnitude. Thus

\[
J(a,b)=\int_a^b\left(\int_0^\infty\exp(-sx)\,dx\right)ds
=\int_a^b\frac1s\,ds=\ln(b/a).
\]

\begin{DomainStep}
\textbf{Step 4 --- DOMAIN.} As \(a\downarrow0\), the answer tends to \(+\infty\). At \(a=0,b>0\), the numerator tends to one at infinity, leaving a nonintegrable \(1/x\) tail. The positive-parameter interchange does not extend to that endpoint.

\end{DomainStep}
\textbf{Step 5 --- CHECK.} \(J(a,a)=0\) and \(J(a,b)=-J(b,a)\). For \(b>a\) the original integrand is nonnegative and the logarithm is positive. Differentiation with respect to \(b>0\) gives \(1/b\), also obtained directly by integrating \(\exp(-bx)\).

\textbf{Discussion.} Cancellation within an integrand can be essential to its existence. Separating the canceled terms before integration can create two undefined quantities.

\section{I4-002 --- A logarithmic kernel on a finite interval}
\label{problem:I4-002}
\markright{I4-002}
{\small Hints: page~\pageref{hints:I4-002}.}\par\medskip

\textbf{Objects and dependencies.} \(x\in[0,1]\) is a real integration variable and \(a>-1\) is an independent real parameter. The real scalar integral is

\[
J(a)=\int_0^1\frac{\ln(1+ax)}{x}\,dx,
\]

with the continuous endpoint value at \(x=0\). Determine its parameter derivative, a convergent series for \(|a|<1\), and a real special-function representation for all \(a>-1\). Decide whether the integral remains finite at \(a=-1\) and whether its derivative has a finite limit there.
\subsection*{Worked solution}

\textbf{Step 1 --- SETUP.} For \(a>-1\), \(1+ax\) remains positive on the interval, and the quotient has limiting value \(a\) as \(x\) tends to zero. The output is real and dimensionless.

\begin{ChoiceStep}
\textbf{Step 2 --- CHOICE.} Parameter differentiation removes the logarithm and supplies a useful representation even though an elementary primitive of the original kernel is unavailable.

\end{ChoiceStep}
\textbf{Step 3 --- CALCULUS.} On a neighborhood of each \(a>-1\), the denominator \(1+ax\) has a positive uniform lower bound. Consequently,

\[
\frac{d}{da}\left(J(a)\right)=\int_0^1\frac1{1+ax}\,dx
=\begin{cases}\ln(1+a)/a,&a\ne0,\\1,&a=0.\end{cases}
\]

The zero-parameter value follows directly from the rational integral, without division by zero.

\begingroup\color{TrickPurple}
\textbf{Step 4 --- TRICK: convergent series.} The logarithm expansion in T-002 is uniformly convergent in \(x\in[0,1]\) for each fixed \(|a|<1\). After division by \(x\), the \(n\)th term has absolute value at most \(|a|^n/n\) on \(0\le x\le1\). The sum of these bounds is finite for \(|a|<1\), so termwise integration is justified. In particular,

\[
\frac{\ln(1+ax)}{x}
=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n+1}a^n x^{n-1}}{n},
\qquad
\int_0^1x^{n-1}\,dx=\frac1n.
\]

Consequently,

\[
J(a)=\sum_{n=1}^\infty\frac{(-1)^{n+1}a^n}{n^2}.
\]

\endgroup
\textbf{Step 5 --- CALCULUS: special-function representation.} For a real scalar \(z<1\), the real dilogarithm is defined by

\[
\operatorname{Li}_2(z)=-\int_0^z\frac{\ln(1-u)}{u}\,du,
\]

where the quotient \(\ln(1-u)/u\) has limiting value \(-1\) at zero. The minus sign outside the integral is part of the definition. The coordinate \(u=-ax\) gives \(J(a)=-\operatorname{Li}_2(-a)\) for \(a\ne0\), and both expressions vanish at \(a=0\). This integral definition also covers \(a>1\), where the displayed power series cannot be used.

\begin{DomainStep}
\textbf{Step 6 --- DOMAIN AND CHECK.} At \(a=-1\), the logarithmic endpoint is integrable: near \(x=1\), \(1/x\) is bounded and \(|\ln(1-x)|\) has a finite integral. For \(a=-r\) with \(0<r<1\), the nonnegative kernels \(-\ln(1-rx)/x\) increase as \(r\) tends to one. Monotone convergence therefore identifies the endpoint integral with the series limit \(J(-1)=-\sum_{n=1}^\infty1/n^2\). However, \(\ln(1+a)/a\to+\infty\) as \(a\downarrow-1\). A finite function value does not provide a finite endpoint parameter derivative. The first series coefficient is one, agreeing with the derivative at zero. The dilogarithm convention follows \href{https://dlmf.nist.gov/25.12}{NIST DLMF 25.12}.

\end{DomainStep}
\textbf{Discussion.} The domain of an exact integral representation can exceed the convergence domain of its local series. Parameter differentiation can strengthen an endpoint singularity.

\section{I4-003 --- A damped difference of oscillatory kernels}
\label{problem:I4-003}
\markright{I4-003}
{\small Hints: page~\pageref{hints:I4-003}.}\par\medskip

\textbf{Objects and dependencies.} \(x\ge0\) is a real integration coordinate. Fixed real parameters satisfy \(a>0\) and \(b,c\ge0\). Determine the real scalar integral

\[
K(a,b,c)=\int_0^\infty\exp(-ax)\frac{\cos(bx)-\cos(cx)}{x}\,dx.
\]

Establish convergence before evaluating it, and give its limit as \(a\downarrow0\) when \(b,c>0\).
\subsection*{Worked solution}

\textbf{Step 1 --- SETUP.} The parameters are fixed inside the integral. Near zero the cosine difference is \((c^2-b^2)x^2/2+O(x^4)\); division by \(x\) leaves an integrable linear behavior. Positive damping ensures absolute convergence at infinity.

\begin{ChoiceStep}
\textbf{Step 2 --- CHOICE.} The derivative with respect to \(c\) has an elementary damped sine integral. The condition \(K(a,b,b)=0\) fixes the parameter integration constant.

\end{ChoiceStep}
\textbf{Step 3 --- CALCULUS.} The parameter derivative integrand is bounded by \(\exp(-ax)\), independently of nearby \(c\), so

\[
\frac{\partial}{\partial c}\left(K(a,b,c)\right)
=\int_0^\infty\exp(-ax)\sin(cx)\,dx=\frac{c}{a^2+c^2}.
\]

The last equality follows by taking the imaginary part of \(\int_0^\infty\exp((-a+\mathrm{i}c)x)\,dx=1/(a-\mathrm{i}c)\), an application of T-001.

\textbf{Step 4 --- CALCULUS AND ALGEBRA.} Integrating in the real frequency coordinate \(s\) from \(b\) to \(c\) gives

\[
K(a,b,c)=\int_b^c\frac{s}{a^2+s^2}\,ds
=\frac12\ln\left(\frac{a^2+c^2}{a^2+b^2}\right).
\]

\begin{DomainStep}
\textbf{Step 5 --- DOMAIN.} For positive \(b,c\), the damping limit is \(\ln(c/b)\). The undamped ordinary integral also exists: integration by parts bounds each cosine tail divided by \(x\) by a constant times \(1/(bA)\) or \(1/(cA)\) beyond \(A>0\). The same bounds are uniform in \(a\ge0\) because \(\exp(-ax)/x\) decreases. Finite-interval convergence and these bounds justify the damping limit. If \(b=0<c\), the limit diverges to \(+\infty\); if \(c=0<b\), it diverges to \(-\infty\). If both vanish, the integral is identically zero.

\end{DomainStep}
\textbf{Step 6 --- CHECK.} Interchanging \(b,c\) negates both the original integrand and the logarithmic answer. At equal frequencies both vanish. The logarithm's argument is positive for \(a>0\), including zero frequencies.

\textbf{Discussion.} A difference of oscillatory kernels can regularize one endpoint while damping controls the other. Each endpoint and each zero-frequency case has a separate convergence argument.

\section{I4-004 --- A weighted logarithm with limiting parameter values}
\label{problem:I4-004}
\markright{I4-004}
{\small Hints: page~\pageref{hints:I4-004}.}\par\medskip

\textbf{Objects and dependencies.} The real integration variable is \(x\in(-1,1)\), and the independent real parameter satisfies \(|\lambda|<1\). Evaluate

\[
J(\lambda)=\int_{-1}^1\frac{\ln(1+\lambda x)}{\sqrt{1-x^2}}\,dx.
\]

Determine whether the integral extends to \(\lambda=\pm1\) and whether differentiation under the integral remains valid at those endpoints.
\subsection*{Worked solution}

\textbf{Step 1 --- SETUP.} For \(|\lambda|<1\), the logarithm has a positive argument throughout the interval. The square-root endpoint singularities are integrable. The output is a real scalar.

\begin{ChoiceStep}
\textbf{Step 2 --- CHOICE.} The denominator \(\sqrt{1-x^2}\) suggests \(x=\cos\theta\) on \(0<\theta<\pi\). On this interval the positive square root is \(\sin\theta\), which cancels the magnitude of the coordinate derivative. Differentiation with respect to \(\lambda\) then removes the logarithm, leaving an angular integral that can be converted to a rational one.

\end{ChoiceStep}
\textbf{Step 3 --- CALCULUS.} The reversed angular bounds and negative coordinate derivative give

\[
J(\lambda)=\int_0^\pi\ln(1+\lambda\cos\theta)\,d\theta,
\qquad
\frac{d}{d\lambda}\left(J(\lambda)\right)=\int_0^\pi\frac{\cos\theta}{1+\lambda\cos\theta}\,d\theta.
\]

For each interior parameter, a local positive lower bound on the denominator justifies the derivative interchange.

\begingroup\color{AlgebraBlue}
\textbf{Step 4 --- ALGEBRA.} For \(\lambda\ne0\), the integrand is \(\lambda^{-1}(1-(1+\lambda\cos\theta)^{-1})\). The real coordinate \(u=\tan(\theta/2)\) has inverse derivative \(2/(1+u^2)\) and bounds zero to infinity. It gives

\[
\int_0^\pi\frac{1}{1+\lambda\cos\theta}\,d\theta
=\int_0^\infty\frac{2}{(1+\lambda)+(1-\lambda)u^2}\,du
=\frac\pi{\sqrt{1-\lambda^2}}.
\]

Both quadratic coefficients are positive on the assumed domain; scaling \(u\) by \(\sqrt{(1-\lambda)/(1+\lambda)}\) reduces the final evaluation to an arctangent.

\endgroup
\textbf{Step 5 --- CALCULUS.} Thus the derivative for nonzero interior \(\lambda\) is \(\pi(1-1/\sqrt{1-\lambda^2})/\lambda\). The condition \(J(0)=0\) fixes its primitive:

\[
J(\lambda)=\pi\ln\left(\frac{1+\sqrt{1-\lambda^2}}{2}\right),\qquad |\lambda|<1.
\]

Differentiation of this expression and multiplication by the conjugate of \(1+\sqrt{1-\lambda^2}\) verifies the derivative.

\begin{DomainStep}
\textbf{Step 6 --- DOMAIN.} At \(\lambda=1\), the angular logarithm near \(\theta=\pi\) behaves as \(2\ln(\pi-\theta)-\ln2\), which is integrable. At \(\lambda=-1\) the same occurs at zero. The limits of the interior integral equal the endpoint integrals: for \(1/2\le\lambda\le1\) and \(0\le\theta<\pi\),

\[
\frac{1+\cos\theta}{2}\le1+\lambda\cos\theta\le2.
\]

It follows that

\[
|\ln(1+\lambda\cos\theta)|
\le\ln2+\left|\ln\left(\frac{1+\cos\theta}{2}\right)\right|.
\]

The right-hand side is integrable and independent of \(\lambda\). Dominated convergence justifies the limit at \(\lambda=1\); reflection gives the limit at \(\lambda=-1\). Both endpoint values are \(-\pi\ln2\). The derivative instead diverges negatively at \(\lambda\uparrow1\) and positively at \(\lambda\downarrow-1\); the derivative kernels are not integrable at the singular endpoint.

\end{DomainStep}
\textbf{Step 7 --- CHECK.} Reflection \(x\mapsto-x\) gives \(J(-\lambda)=J(\lambda)\). The answer is even and vanishes at zero. Expansion gives \(J(\lambda)=-\pi\lambda^2/4+O(\lambda^4)\), agreeing with the first nonzero term obtained by integrating the logarithm series against the even weight.

\textbf{Discussion.} A geometric weight may identify a coordinate, while a parameter derivative identifies a second reduction. Existence of endpoint integrals does not establish endpoint derivative interchange.

\section{I4-005 --- Flux through a translated ellipsoid}
\label{problem:I4-005}
\markright{I4-005}
{\small Hints: page~\pageref{hints:I4-005}.}\par\medskip

\textbf{Objects and dependencies.} Fixed real scalars \(a,b,c>0\) and \(d\in\mathbb R\), with \(d\ne\pm a\), define the bounded volume

\[
V_d=\left\{(x,y,z):\frac{(x-d)^2}{a^2}+\frac{y^2}{b^2}+\frac{z^2}{c^2}\le1\right\}.
\]

The independent spatial coordinates are real. The real vector field is \(\mathbf F(x,y,z)=[x,y,z]^{\mathsf T}/(x^2+y^2+z^2)^{3/2}\) away from the origin. Determine its flux through \(\partial V_d\) with outward orientation, and the two limits as \(d\) approaches \(a\) from opposite sides.
\subsection*{Worked solution}

\textbf{Step 1 --- SETUP.} The surface is smooth and compact. The origin is not on it because \(d\ne\pm a\), so the real scalar flux exists as an ordinary surface integral. The source is inside precisely when \(d^2/a^2<1\).

\begin{ChoiceStep}
\textbf{Step 2 --- CHOICE.} The geometric placement of the excluded source determines whether the classical divergence theorem applies directly. Surface parameterization would introduce unnecessary algebra without resolving that regularity issue.

\end{ChoiceStep}
\textbf{Step 3 --- CALCULUS.} The distance from the origin is \(R(x,y,z)=\sqrt{x^2+y^2+z^2}>0\). Coordinate differentiation gives

\[
\nabla\cdot\mathbf F(x,y,z)
=\frac{3}{R(x,y,z)^3}-\frac{3(x^2+y^2+z^2)}{R(x,y,z)^5}=0.
\]

For \(|d|>a\), the field is smooth on a neighborhood of \(V_d\) and the flux is zero.

\begin{DomainStep}
\textbf{Step 4 --- DOMAIN: excision of the source.} For \(|d|<a\), a ball of sufficiently small positive radius \(\epsilon\) fits inside \(V_d\). On the punctured volume the divergence theorem gives

\[
0=\iint_{\partial V_d}\mathbf F(x,y,z)\cdot\mathbf n(x,y,z)\,dS
-\iint_{R(x,y,z)=\epsilon}\mathbf F(x,y,z)\cdot\mathbf n_{\rm radial}(x,y,z)\,dS.
\]

The inner boundary has the negative radial orientation relative to the punctured volume. On its sphere, the radial field component is \(1/\epsilon^2\) and the area is \(4\pi\epsilon^2\), so the outward ellipsoid flux is \(4\pi\).

\end{DomainStep}
\textbf{Step 5 --- RESULT AND CHECK.} The flux is \(4\pi\) for \(|d|<a\) and zero for \(|d|>a\). Consequently the limits at \(d=a\) are \(4\pi\) from below and zero from above. A continuous deformation preserving the source placement preserves the answer. At \(d=a\) the source reaches the surface and the ordinary smooth-field hypotheses used above fail; this excluded case is not assigned either value by substitution. The point-source flux principle is documented in \href{https://ocw.mit.edu/ans7870/18/18.013a/textbook/HTML/chapter28/section04.html}{MIT, Electrostatics and Potentials}.

\textbf{Discussion.} A zero classical divergence away from a source does not imply zero flux through a surface enclosing that source. Domain topology and orientation can replace a difficult explicit surface integral.

\section{I4-006 --- A panel kernel at positive observation distance}
\label{problem:I4-006}
\markright{I4-006}
{\small Hints: page~\pageref{hints:I4-006}.}\par\medskip

\textbf{Objects and dependencies.} Independent real dimensionless coordinates satisfy \(0\le y\le x\le1\). The independent real observation parameter is \(h>0\). Evaluate the real scalar integral

\[
K(h)=\int_0^1\int_0^x\frac{1}{\sqrt{x^2+y^2+h^2}}\,dy\,dx.
\]

Determine its continuous limit at \(h=0\), its right derivative there, and an explicit bound on the error in its linear small-\(h\) approximation. Explain whether the limit of the parameter derivative may be passed through the original area integral.
\subsection*{Worked solution}

\textbf{Step 1 --- SETUP.} For each \(h>0\) the kernel is real and continuous on the closed triangle. The output is a real scalar. The limiting kernel at zero has the integrable vertex singularity studied in I3-007.

\begin{ChoiceStep}
\textbf{Step 2 --- CHOICE.} A triangle-to-square map exposes the area Jacobian and permits the radial coordinate to be integrated first. The remaining expression supports both exact evaluation and a uniform small-distance estimate.

\end{ChoiceStep}
\textbf{Step 3 --- CALCULUS.} With real \(x(u,v)=u\), \(y(u,v)=uv\) on the unit square, the absolute Jacobian is \(u\). Integration in \(u\) gives

\[
K(h)=\int_0^1\int_0^1\frac{u}{\sqrt{u^2(1+v^2)+h^2}}\,du\,dv
=\int_0^1\frac{\sqrt{1+v^2+h^2}-h}{1+v^2}\,dv.
\]

The lower radial endpoint contributes \(h\), since \(h>0\).

\begingroup\color{TrickPurple}
\textbf{Step 4 --- TRICK: hyperbolic representation.} The abbreviation \(A=1+h^2\) denotes a positive scalar that is fixed during integration in \(v\). The coordinate \(v=\sqrt A\sinh t\) gives

\[
\frac{\sqrt{A+v^2}}{1+v^2}\,dv
=\frac{A\cosh^2t}{1+A\sinh^2t}\,dt
=\left(1+\frac{h^2}{1+A\sinh^2t}\right)dt.
\]

The identities are those of T-007. The additional coordinate \(w=\tanh t\) satisfies

\[
\frac{d}{dt}(w(t))=1-w(t)^2,
\qquad
\sinh^2t=\frac{w^2}{1-w^2}.
\]

Thus the remaining integral becomes

\[
\begin{aligned}
\int\frac{h^2}{1+A\sinh^2t}\,dt
&=\int\frac{h^2}{1+A w^2/(1-w^2)}\frac{1}{1-w^2}\,dw\\
&=\int\frac{h^2}{1+(A-1)w^2}\,dw\\
&=\int\frac{h^2}{1+h^2w^2}\,dw
=h\arctan(hw)+C.
\end{aligned}
\]

Here \(h>0\) is fixed during integration, and \(C\) is independent of \(w\).

\endgroup
\textbf{Step 5 --- CALCULUS.} Since \(t=\operatorname{arsinh}(v/\sqrt A)\) and \(w=v/\sqrt{A+v^2}\), endpoint evaluation gives

\[
K(h)=\operatorname{arsinh}\left(\frac1{\sqrt{1+h^2}}\right)
+h\arctan\left(\frac{h}{\sqrt{2+h^2}}\right)-\frac{\pi h}{4}.
\]

\begingroup\color{AlgebraBlue}
\textbf{Step 6 --- ALGEBRA: a controlled remainder.} A more transparent form for small \(h\) is

\[
K(h)=\ln(1+\sqrt2)-\frac{\pi h}{4}+E(h),
\]

where rationalizing the difference of roots yields

\[
\begin{aligned}
E(h)&=\int_0^1\frac{h^2}{(1+v^2)(\sqrt{1+v^2+h^2}+\sqrt{1+v^2})}\,dv,\\
0\le E(h)&\le\frac{h^2}{2}\int_0^1(1+v^2)^{-3/2}\,dv
=\frac{h^2}{2\sqrt2}.
\end{aligned}
\]

\endgroup
\begin{DomainStep}
\textbf{Step 7 --- DOMAIN AND CHECK.} The extension has \(K(0)=\ln(1+\sqrt2)\) and right derivative \(-\pi/4\). At each positive \(h\), differentiation under the original finite area integral is valid and its kernel derivative is \(-h(x^2+y^2+h^2)^{-3/2}\). At every nonzero spatial point that derivative tends to zero as \(h\downarrow0\). Passing that pointwise limit through the area integral would give zero, contradicting the established slope. No integrable domination uniform through the source limit is available. Direct quadrature at fixed positive distances verifies the closed form; the remainder bound verifies the endpoint derivative without differentiating an asymptotic error symbol.

\end{DomainStep}
\textbf{Discussion.} Near-singular parameter limits can concentrate a derivative in an arbitrarily small spatial region. The coordinate reduction makes that contribution visible.

Evaluate \(\int_0^\pi q(x)\,dx\). Assess whether the coordinate \(u=g(x)=\sin x\) permits evaluation over one transformed interval without a branch partition.

\textbf{Step 1 --- SETUP.} The input and integration variable is the real angle \(x\in[0,\pi]\), and the output integral is a real number. The function \(\sin x\) is continuous throughout this interval.

\begin{ChoiceStep}
\textbf{Step 2 --- CHOICE.} The map \(g(x)=\sin x\) is not one-to-one on the full interval. The substitution rule for an integrand \(h(g(x))\frac{d}{dx}(g(x))\) cannot be applied directly: the original integrand is \(\sin x\), not \(h(\sin x)\cos x\) for a single globally defined \(h\) here. An inverse-coordinate substitution instead needs two branches.

\end{ChoiceStep}
\textbf{Step 3 --- CALCULUS.} On \(0\le x<\pi/2\), the inverse is \(x_1(u)=\arcsin u\), with derivative \(1/\sqrt{1-u^2}\) and limits \(u=0\) to \(u=1\). On \(\pi/2<x\le\pi\), the inverse is \(x_2(u)=\pi-\arcsin u\), with derivative \(-1/\sqrt{1-u^2}\) and limits \(u=1\) to \(u=0\). \[
\int_0^\pi\sin x\,dx
=\int_0^1\frac{u}{\sqrt{1-u^2}}\,du
+\int_1^0\frac{-u}{\sqrt{1-u^2}}\,du.
\]

\begingroup\color{AlgebraBlue}

\[
\int_0^\pi\sin x\,dx
=2\lim_{b\uparrow1}\left(-\sqrt{1-b^2}+1\right)=2.

By contrast, \(\int_0^\pi\sin x\cos x\,dx=0\) legitimately follows from the direct chain-rule substitution, which does not require an inverse map.

\textbf{Discussion.} Equal transformed endpoints are meaningful only after the transformed integrand and the applicable substitution theorem have been identified. A folding coordinate map can conceal multiplicity and orientation.

\begin{ChoiceStep}
\textbf{Step 2 --- CHOICE.} The numerator's derivative does not match a substitution that turns this into an elementary sine primitive. The ratio leads to a special function instead. For \(k\ne0\), \(u=g(x;k)=kx\) is a valid invertible linear coordinate, with real scalar inverse \(x=h(u;k)=u/k\).

\end{ChoiceStep}
\textbf{Step 3 --- CALCULUS.} With \(k\) held fixed, the coordinate derivatives are \(\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\left(g(x;k)\right)=k\) and \(\frac{\partial}{\partial u}\left(h(u;k)\right)=1/k\). The transformed bounds are \(u=0\) to \(u=kL\). Thus

J(k,L)=\int_0^{kL}\frac{\sin u}{u}\,du=\operatorname{Si}(kL).
\]

\begingroup\color{TrickPurple}
\textbf{Step 4 --- TRICK: Taylor expansion with remainder.} The supporting expansion and error convention are described in T-002. For real \(k,x\), Taylor's theorem gives

\[
\sin(kx)=kx-\frac{(kx)^3}{6}+r(x;k),\qquad |r(x;k)|\le\frac{|kx|^5}{120}.
\]

Dividing by \(x>0\) and integrating over the finite interval gives

\[
J(k,L)=kL-\frac{k^3L^3}{18}+E(k,L),\qquad
|E(k,L)|\le\frac{|k|^5L^5}{600}.
\]

\endgroup
\textbf{Step 5 --- CHECK.} With respect to the real endpoint \(L\), the fundamental theorem gives \(\frac{\partial}{\partial L}(J(k,L))=\sin(kL)/L\) for \(L>0\). This agrees with the special-function expression. The integral is odd in \(k\), and the approximation preserves that property. Small \(|kL|\) controls the stated error; a small value of \(k\) alone is insufficient when \(L\) is large.

\textbf{Discussion.} A familiar sine inside a ratio need not have an elementary antiderivative. A special-function definition and a controlled approximation are valid, distinct forms of a solution.

\section{I3-003 --- Damping a conditionally convergent oscillatory integral}
\label{problem:I3-003}
\markright{I3-003}
{\small Hints: page~\pageref{hints:I3-003}.}\par\medskip

\textbf{Objects and dependencies.} \(x\ge0\) is the real integration coordinate; \(k>0\) is a fixed real wavenumber. The real scalar damping parameter \(\epsilon>0\) has the same units as \(k\). The scalar integrand has its removable value \(k\) at zero. Define

\[
F(\epsilon;k)=\int_0^\infty \exp(-\epsilon x)\frac{\sin(kx)}{x}\,dx.
\]

Evaluate \(F(\epsilon;k)\) using differentiation with respect to \(\epsilon\). Determine the ordinary improper integral with \(\epsilon=0\), and distinguish its convergence from absolute convergence. Any interchange of a derivative or a limit with the integral requires a stated justification.
\subsection*{Worked solution}

\textbf{Step 1 --- SETUP.} The integration coordinate is real \(x\ge0\), while \(k>0\) is fixed and \(\epsilon>0\) varies. The integrand is bounded at zero after extension and is absolutely integrable at infinity when \(\epsilon>0\).

\begin{ChoiceStep}
\textbf{Step 2 --- CHOICE.} Differentiation with respect to damping removes \(1/x\). Near any fixed \(\epsilon_0>0\), the absolute value of the differentiated integrand is bounded by \(\exp(-\epsilon_0x/2)\) for \(\epsilon\ge\epsilon_0/2\). This integrable bound justifies differentiation under the integral sign locally.

\end{ChoiceStep}
\textbf{Step 3 --- CALCULUS.} With \(k\) held fixed,

\[
\frac{\partial}{\partial\epsilon}\left(F(\epsilon;k)\right)
=-\int_0^\infty\exp(-\epsilon x)\sin(kx)\,dx.
\]

\begingroup\color{TrickPurple}
\textbf{Step 4 --- TRICK: Euler identity.} The supporting identity is listed in T-001. The imaginary part of \(\exp((-\epsilon+\mathrm{i}k)x)\) is \(\exp(-\epsilon x)\sin(kx)\). Since \(\epsilon>0\), its boundary value at infinity is zero, and

\[
\int_0^\infty\exp((-\epsilon+\mathrm{i}k)x)\,dx
=\frac{1}{\epsilon-\mathrm{i}k}
=\frac{\epsilon+\mathrm{i}k}{\epsilon^2+k^2}.
\]

The imaginary part is \(k/(\epsilon^2+k^2)\).

\endgroup
\textbf{Step 5 --- CALCULUS.} Integration with respect to the real damping parameter gives \(F(\epsilon;k)=-\arctan(\epsilon/k)+C(k)\), where the scalar \(C(k)\) is independent of \(\epsilon\).

\begingroup\color{AlgebraBlue}
\textbf{Step 6 --- ALGEBRA.} The estimate \(|\sin(kx)|/x\le k\) gives \(|F(\epsilon;k)|\le k/\epsilon\), hence \(F\to0\) as \(\epsilon\to\infty\). Therefore

\[
F(\epsilon;k)=\frac{\pi}{2}-\arctan(\epsilon/k)=\arctan(k/\epsilon).
\]

\endgroup
\begin{DomainStep}
\textbf{Step 7 --- DOMAIN: the undamped limit.} Absolute domination by \(|\sin(kx)|/x\) is unavailable on the infinite interval. For any real \(A>0\), let \(f_\epsilon(x)=\exp(-\epsilon x)/x\), including \(\epsilon=0\). This positive function decreases to zero. Integration by parts against the bounded primitive \(-\cos(kx)/k\) gives, for \(B>A\),

\[
\left|\int_A^B f_\epsilon(x)\sin(kx)\,dx\right|
\le\frac{f_\epsilon(A)+f_\epsilon(B)+\int_A^B|\frac{d}{dx}(f_\epsilon(x))|\,dx}{k}
=\frac{2f_\epsilon(A)}{k}\le\frac{2}{kA}.
\]

The same uniform tail bound holds as \(B\to\infty\). On the finite interval \([0,A]\), the integrands converge under a bounded domination as \(\epsilon\downarrow0\). Finite-interval convergence plus the uniform tail bound justifies the limit:

\[
\int_0^\infty\frac{\sin(kx)}{x}\,dx
=\lim_{\epsilon\downarrow0}F(\epsilon;k)=\frac{\pi}{2},\qquad k>0.
\]

\end{DomainStep}
\textbf{Step 8 --- CHECK: absolute convergence fails.} In the coordinate \(u=kx\), each interval \([n\pi+\pi/6,n\pi+5\pi/6]\) has \(|\sin u|\ge1/2\). For integers \(n\ge1\), its contribution to \(\int|\sin u|/u\,du\) is at least \(1/[3(n+1)]\). The harmonic lower bound diverges. Thus the undamped integral is conditionally convergent. Its differentiated damping integral at \(\epsilon=0\) would contain \(\int_0^\infty\sin(kx)\,dx\), whose ordinary limit does not exist. The interchange valid for positive damping is not valid at the endpoint.

\textbf{Discussion.} Oscillatory cancellation can make an integral converge without absolute convergence. A limit at the edge of a parameter domain needs its own argument.

\label{problem:I3-005}
\markright{I3-005}
{\small Hints: page~\pageref{hints:I3-005}.}\par\medskip

\textbf{Objects and dependencies.} \(x\) is a dimensionless real integration coordinate on \([-1,1]\). A fixed real scalar \(a\) satisfies \(-1<a<1\). The real scalar function \(q(x;a)=1/(x-a)\) is undefined at \(x=a\). Determine whether \(\int_{-1}^1q(x;a)\,dx\) exists as an ordinary improper integral, and evaluate its Cauchy principal value with equal exclusions \(a-\delta,a+\delta\) for real \(\delta>0\) small enough. Describe what happens when \(a\) is an endpoint.
\subsection*{Worked solution}

\textbf{Step 1 --- SETUP.} \(x\) is the integration variable; the fixed real pole location \(a\) is inside \((-1,1)\). The scalar function is continuous on each side of \(a\) but not at \(a\).

\begin{ChoiceStep}
\textbf{Step 2 --- CHOICE.} The ordinary improper integral requires two separate finite limits. The antiderivative \(\ln|x-a|\) is valid on each connected side, and does not justify crossing the pole in one endpoint subtraction.

\end{ChoiceStep}
\textbf{Step 3 --- CALCULUS.} For positive real cutoffs \(\delta_-,\delta_+\),

\[
\begin{aligned}
\int_{-1}^{a-\delta_-}\frac{1}{x-a}\,dx&=\ln\delta_- -\ln(1+a)\longrightarrow-\infty,\\
\int_{a+\delta_+}^{1}\frac{1}{x-a}\,dx&=\ln(1-a)-\ln\delta_+\longrightarrow+\infty.
\end{aligned}
\]

The ordinary integral does not exist. The expression \(-\infty+\infty\) has no assigned value.

\begingroup\color{AlgebraBlue}
\textbf{Step 4 --- ALGEBRA: the specified principal value.} Setting both cutoffs equal to a real scalar \(\delta\) before taking the limit gives

\[
\operatorname{PV}\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{1}{x-a}\,dx
=\lim_{\delta\downarrow0}\left(\ln\delta-\ln(1+a)+\ln(1-a)-\ln\delta\right)
=\ln\left(\frac{1-a}{1+a}\right).
\]

The ratio is positive for \(-1<a<1\).

\endgroup
\textbf{Step 5 --- CHECK.} At \(a=0\), the principal value is zero by odd symmetry. If instead \(\delta_-=\lambda\delta_+\) with a fixed real \(\lambda>0\), the coupled limit differs by \(\ln\lambda\). This shows why a limiting prescription matters. At \(a=1\) or \(a=-1\), only a one-sided endpoint divergence remains and the stated interior symmetric principal value is unavailable. A finite-part regularization would be another explicitly defined operation.

\textbf{Discussion.} Cancellation of divergences is a prescription, not proof that an ordinary improper integral exists. Symmetry is useful only after the definition of the integral is fixed.

\section{I3-006 --- Near a line source: differentiation under an integral}
\label{problem:I3-006}
\markright{I3-006}
{\small Hints: page~\pageref{hints:I3-006}.}\par\medskip

\textbf{Objects and dependencies.} \(s\in[-L,L]\) is a real scalar source coordinate, with fixed real length \(L>0\). The independent real scalar observation distance is \(\rho>0\). The real dimensionless scalar kernel integral is

\[
P(\rho;L)=\int_{-L}^L\frac{1}{\sqrt{\rho^2+s^2}}\,ds.
\]

Evaluate \(P(\rho;L)\), find \(\frac{\partial}{\partial\rho}\left(P(\rho;L)\right)\) with \(L\) held fixed both from the closed form and under the integral sign, and describe the limit \(\rho\downarrow0\). A constant charge and permittivity prefactor would convert \(P\) into an electrostatic potential.
\subsection*{Worked solution}

\textbf{Step 1 --- SETUP.} The source coordinate \(s\) is integrated over the fixed finite interval \([-L,L]\). The parameter \(\rho>0\) is a real distance, and \(P(\rho;L)\) is a real dimensionless scalar. The kernel is continuous for every such parameter.

\begin{ChoiceStep}
\textbf{Step 2 --- CHOICE.} The hyperbolic coordinate \(s=h(u;\rho)=\rho\sinh u\) is one-to-one for fixed \(\rho>0\). It turns \(\sqrt{\rho^2+s^2}\) into \(\rho\cosh u\); positivity prevents an absolute-value ambiguity.

\end{ChoiceStep}
\textbf{Step 3 --- CALCULUS.} Holding \(\rho\) fixed during the substitution,

\[
\frac{\partial}{\partial u}\left(h(u;\rho)\right)=\rho\cosh u,\qquad
u_-=-\operatorname{arsinh}(L/\rho),\quad u_+=\operatorname{arsinh}(L/\rho).
\]

The integral is therefore

\[
P(\rho;L)=\int_{u_-}^{u_+}1\,du=2\operatorname{arsinh}(L/\rho)
=2\ln\left(\frac{L+\sqrt{L^2+\rho^2}}{\rho}\right).
\]

The logarithm's argument is dimensionless and positive. The bounds in the transformed coordinate depend on \(\rho\) even though the original bounds do not.

\textbf{Step 4 --- CALCULUS.} Differentiating the closed form with \(L\) fixed gives

\[
\frac{\partial}{\partial\rho}\left(P(\rho;L)\right)
=\frac{2}{\sqrt{1+(L/\rho)^2}}\left(-\frac{L}{\rho^2}\right)
=-\frac{2L}{\rho\sqrt{\rho^2+L^2}}.
\]

\textbf{Step 5 --- CALCULUS: differentiation under the integral.} On a neighborhood of any fixed \(\rho_0>0\), the kernel and its \(\rho\) derivative are continuous and bounded on the finite source interval. This justifies

\[
\frac{\partial}{\partial\rho}\left(P(\rho;L)\right)
=-\int_{-L}^{L}\frac{\rho}{(\rho^2+s^2)^{3/2}}\,ds.
\]

The derivative with respect to \(s\) of \(s/\sqrt{\rho^2+s^2}\) is \(\rho^2/(\rho^2+s^2)^{3/2}\), with \(\rho\) fixed. Endpoint evaluation thus gives

\[
-\frac{1}{\rho}\left[\frac{s}{\sqrt{\rho^2+s^2}}\right]_{s=-L}^{s=L}
=-\frac{2L}{\rho\sqrt{\rho^2+L^2}}.
\]

The square brackets here delimit endpoint evaluation, not a derivative operand or an object type.

\begin{DomainStep}
\textbf{Step 6 --- CHECK AND DOMAIN.} For small positive \(\rho/L\), the potential grows like \(2\ln(2L/\rho)\) and its derivative like \(-2/\rho\). At \(\rho=0\) the original kernel is \(1/|s|\) and its integral diverges. The positive-distance justification is not uniform through the line source. For large \(\rho/L\), \(P(\rho;L)\) behaves like \(2L/\rho\), as expected from the nearly constant kernel across the short source segment.

\end{DomainStep}
\textbf{Discussion.} A near-source limit can invalidate interchange rules that are harmless at every fixed positive observation distance. Parameter-dependent transformed bounds cannot be treated as constants in a later derivative.

\]

\begin{ChoiceStep}
\textbf{Step 2 --- CHOICE.} The new coordinate \(u\) measures distance toward the singular vertex while \(v\) describes direction inside the triangle. The Jacobian may compensate the kernel's singularity. The edge \(u=0\) collapses to the vertex and is handled by a limit.

\end{ChoiceStep}
\textbf{Step 3 --- CALCULUS.} The real \(2\times2\) coordinate Jacobian is

\[
\mathbf J(u,v)=\begin{bmatrix}
\frac{\partial}{\partial u}(x(u,v))&\frac{\partial}{\partial v}(x(u,v))\\
\frac{\partial}{\partial u}(y(u,v))&\frac{\partial}{\partial v}(y(u,v))
\end{bmatrix}
=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\v&u\end{bmatrix},\qquad |\det\mathbf J(u,v)|=u.
\]

\begingroup\color{AlgebraBlue}
\textbf{Step 4 --- ALGEBRA.} Since \(u>0\), the distance is \(\sqrt{u^2+u^2v^2}=u\sqrt{1+v^2}\). On the truncated triangle \(x\ge\delta>0\), both kernel and Jacobian are regular. Their product gives

\[
K=\lim_{\delta\downarrow0}\int_\delta^1\int_0^1
\frac{u}{u\sqrt{1+v^2}}\,dv\,du
=\int_0^1\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+v^2}}\,dv
=\operatorname{arsinh}(1)=\ln(1+\sqrt2).
\]

Nonnegativity ensures that this increasing truncation gives the improper area integral, independent of the way nested neighborhoods of the vertex are removed.

\endgroup
\begin{DomainStep}
\textbf{Step 5 --- DOMAIN.} For the stronger scalar kernel \(1/(x^2+y^2)\), the transformed integral is instead

\[
\lim_{\delta\downarrow0}\int_\delta^1\frac{1}{u}\,du\int_0^1\frac{1}{1+v^2}\,dv
=\lim_{\delta\downarrow0}(-\ln\delta)\frac{\pi}{4}=+\infty.
\]

No oscillatory or signed cancellation is available in this nonnegative example.

\end{DomainStep}
\textbf{Step 6 --- CHECK.} The transformed \(1/R\) integrand is bounded between \(1/\sqrt2\) and one on a unit square, so the answer lies between those numbers; \(\ln(1+\sqrt2)\) does. This vertex example illustrates the Duffy coordinate idea used in singular panel integration. More general CEM kernels, interior source positions, and vector testing functions require their own transformations and convergence analysis.

\textbf{Discussion.} The word singular does not determine integrability. The kernel, the dimension of the integration measure, and the coordinate Jacobian must be considered together.

\section{I3-008 --- An ellipsoidal weighted volume}
\label{problem:I3-008}
\markright{I3-008}
{\small Hints: page~\pageref{hints:I3-008}.}\par\medskip

\textbf{Objects and dependencies.} Fixed real lengths \(a,b,c>0\) define the ellipsoid \(E=\{(x,y,z):x^2/a^2+y^2/b^2+z^2/c^2\le1\}\). The independent real coordinates are \(x,y,z\). Define the real dimensionless scalar \(q(x,y,z)=x^2/a^2+y^2/b^2+z^2/c^2\). Evaluate the real scalar weighted volume

\[
W(a,b,c)=\iiint_E\exp(-q(x,y,z))\,dx\,dy\,dz.
\]

The error function, if needed, is defined for real scalar \(s\) by \(\operatorname{erf}(s)=\frac{2}{\sqrt\pi}\int_0^s\exp(-t^2)\,dt\), where \(t\) is a dummy real integration variable. Show the transformed domain and the volume Jacobian, not only the transformed exponential.
\subsection*{Worked solution}

\textbf{Step 1 --- SETUP.} The independent Cartesian coordinates are real. The integrand is a positive scalar and the answer has units of volume. All semiaxes are fixed and positive, so the domain is compact with no integrand singularity.

\begin{ChoiceStep}
\textbf{Step 2 --- CHOICE.} The ellipsoidal quadratic form suggests scaling to the unit ball, followed by spherical coordinates. The transformed domain and measure are changed along with the function.

\end{ChoiceStep}
\textbf{Step 3 --- CALCULUS: coordinate map.} Independent real coordinates satisfy \(0\le r\le1\), \(0\le\theta\le\pi\), \(0\le\varphi<2\pi\). Define

\[
\begin{aligned}
x(r,\theta,\varphi)&=ar\sin\theta\cos\varphi,\\
y(r,\theta,\varphi)&=br\sin\theta\sin\varphi,\\
z(r,\theta,\varphi)&=cr\cos\theta.
\end{aligned}
\]

The real \(3\times3\) Jacobian has columns given by partial differentiation with respect to \(r,\theta,\varphi\):

\[
\mathbf J(r,\theta,\varphi)=\begin{bmatrix}
a\sin\theta\cos\varphi&ar\cos\theta\cos\varphi&-ar\sin\theta\sin\varphi\\
b\sin\theta\sin\varphi&br\cos\theta\sin\varphi&br\sin\theta\cos\varphi\\
c\cos\theta&-cr\sin\theta&0
\end{bmatrix}.
\]

\begingroup\color{AlgebraBlue}
\textbf{Step 4 --- ALGEBRA.} Factoring \(a,b,c\) from the rows and \(r\) from each of the last two columns leaves determinant \(\sin\theta\). Thus \(|\det\mathbf J|=abc\,r^2\sin\theta\). Substitution into \(q\) gives

\[
q(x(r,\theta,\varphi),y(r,\theta,\varphi),z(r,\theta,\varphi))
=r^2\bigl(\sin^2\theta(\cos^2\varphi+\sin^2\varphi)+\cos^2\theta\bigr)=r^2.
\]

The coordinate singularities at the center, poles, and seam occupy sets of zero volume; the map is regular and one-to-one in the interior away from those sets.

\endgroup
\textbf{Step 5 --- CALCULUS.} The full transformed integral is

\[
W(a,b,c)=abc\int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^\pi\int_0^1\exp(-r^2)r^2\sin\theta\,dr\,d\theta\,d\varphi
=4\pi abc\int_0^1r^2\exp(-r^2)\,dr.
\]

For integration by parts in \(r\), the factor \(r\exp(-r^2)\) has primitive \(-(1/2)\exp(-r^2)\), while the remaining factor \(r\) has derivative one. Therefore

\[
\int_0^1r^2\exp(-r^2)\,dr
=-\frac{1}{2\exp(1)}+\frac12\int_0^1\exp(-r^2)\,dr
=-\frac{1}{2\exp(1)}+\frac{\sqrt\pi}{4}\operatorname{erf}(1).
\]

\textbf{Step 6 --- RESULT AND CHECK.} The weighted volume is

\[
W(a,b,c)=abc\left(\pi^{3/2}\operatorname{erf}(1)-\frac{2\pi}{\exp(1)}\right).
\]

On the ellipsoid, \(0\le q\le1\), so the result must lie between \(4\pi abc/(3\exp(1))\) and the unweighted volume \(4\pi abc/3\). The dimensionless coefficient is approximately \(2.381\), within those bounds. A determinant without its absolute value would incorrectly make volume depend on orientation if a coordinate direction were reversed.

\textbf{Discussion.} A nonlinear integrand can become radial after a linear geometric change. The Jacobian supplies the volume scale and the radial power; dropping either changes the problem.

\textbf{Step 2 --- CHOICE.} The area is computed from the tangent cross product rather than from a volume Jacobian or a single height graph. The calculation in D3-005 applies with \(a=c=\ell\).

\end{ChoiceStep}
\textbf{Step 3 --- CALCULUS.} The two tangent vectors are

\[
\begin{aligned}
\frac{\partial}{\partial u}\left(\mathbf r(u,v)\right)&=\ell\begin{bmatrix}\sinh u\cos v\\\sinh u\sin v\\\cosh u\end{bmatrix},\\
\frac{\partial}{\partial v}\left(\mathbf r(u,v)\right)&=\ell\begin{bmatrix}-\cosh u\sin v\\\cosh u\cos v\\0\end{bmatrix}.
\end{aligned}
\]

Their cross product is \(\ell^2[-\cosh^2u\cos v,-\cosh^2u\sin v,\sinh u\cosh u]^{\mathsf T}\), so its length is \(\ell^2\cosh u\sqrt{\cosh^2u+\sinh^2u}\). The length is positive for every finite \(u\).

\begingroup\color{TrickPurple}
\textbf{Step 4 --- TRICK: hyperbolic identity.} The real identities are listed in T-007. Since \(\cosh^2u+\sinh^2u=1+2\sinh^2u\),

\[
S(U;\ell)=2\pi\ell^2\int_{-U}^U\cosh u\sqrt{1+2\sinh^2u}\,du.
\]

The coordinate \(t=g(u)=\sinh u\) is globally increasing, with derivative \(\cosh u>0\). Its bounds are \(-T,T\), where the fixed real scalar \(T=\sinh U\ge0\).

\endgroup
\textbf{Step 5 --- CALCULUS.} The transformed integral is \(2\pi\ell^2\int_{-T}^T\sqrt{1+2t^2}\,dt\). A further real coordinate \(w\) with \(t=h(w)=\sinh w/\sqrt2\) gives \(\frac{d}{dw}\left(h(w)\right)=\cosh w/\sqrt2\) and \(\sqrt{1+2t^2}=\cosh w\). The identity \(\cosh^2w=(1+\cosh(2w))/2\) then gives the primitive

\[
B(t)=\frac{t}{2}\sqrt{1+2t^2}+\frac{1}{2\sqrt2}\operatorname{arsinh}(\sqrt2t).
\]

\begingroup\color{AlgebraBlue}
\textbf{Step 6 --- ALGEBRA.} The function \(B(t)\) is odd. Endpoint subtraction yields

\[
S(U;\ell)=2\pi\ell^2\left(T\sqrt{1+2T^2}+\frac{\operatorname{arsinh}(\sqrt2T)}{\sqrt2}\right),\qquad T=\sinh U.
\]

\endgroup
\begin{DomainStep}
\textbf{Step 7 --- CHECK AND DOMAIN.} Differentiation of \(B(t)\) with respect to \(t\) gives \(\sqrt{1+2t^2}\), verifying the primitive. At \(U=0\) the area is zero, and for small \(U\) it behaves as \(4\pi\ell^2U\). As \(U\to\infty\), \(T\to\infty\) and already \(T\sqrt{1+2T^2}\to\infty\), so the complete surface has infinite area. The finite-band calculation cannot supply a finite area for the unbounded surface by leaving off its limits.

\end{DomainStep}
\textbf{Discussion.} A regular surface can have infinite total area. Coordinate smoothness and convergence of an integral over a noncompact domain answer different questions.

\section{T-001 Euler identities}

\textbf{Objects and conditions.} \(\theta\in\mathbb R\) is an independent real scalar
angle, measured in radians. \(\mathrm i\) is the fixed imaginary scalar with
\(\mathrm i^2=-1\). A complex scalar \(z=a+\mathrm i b\) has real scalar parts \(a,b\);
\(\operatorname{Re}(z)=a\) and \(\operatorname{Im}(z)=b\).
The complex exponential \(\exp(z)\) is a scalar-valued function of a complex scalar
input. Sine and cosine of real angles are real scalars.
The usual notation \(e^z\) means the same function as \(\exp(z)\), where
\(e=\exp(1)\) is the base of the natural logarithm. The form \(\exp(z)\) keeps the
input visible.

\textbf{TRICK --- exact identities.}

\[
\exp(\mathrm i\theta)=\cos(\theta)+\mathrm i\sin(\theta),
\qquad
\exp(-\mathrm i\theta)=\cos(\theta)-\mathrm i\sin(\theta).
\]

Adding and subtracting these equations gives

\[
\cos(\theta)=\frac{\exp(\mathrm i\theta)+\exp(-\mathrm i\theta)}{2},
\qquad
\sin(\theta)=\frac{\exp(\mathrm i\theta)-\exp(-\mathrm i\theta)}{2\mathrm i}.
\]

At \(\theta=\pi\), where \(\pi\) is the fixed real circle constant,
\(\cos(\pi)=-1\) and \(\sin(\pi)=0\), so \(\exp(\mathrm i\pi)+1=0\).
These formulas are recorded in \href{https://dlmf.nist.gov/4.2}{NIST DLMF §4.2}.

\subsection{Worked use: derive double-angle identities}

\textbf{Step 1 --- SETUP.} The same real scalar \(\theta\) supplies every angle below.
All expressions containing \(\mathrm i\) are complex scalars.

\begingroup\color{TrickPurple}
\textbf{Step 2 --- TRICK: the exponential addition identity.} The complex
exponential satisfies \(\exp(z+w)=\exp(z)\exp(w)\) for complex scalar inputs \(z,w\).
With \(z=w=\mathrm i\theta\),

\[
\exp(2\mathrm i\theta)
=\exp(\mathrm i\theta)\exp(\mathrm i\theta)
=\left(\cos(\theta)+\mathrm i\sin(\theta)\right)^2.
\]

\endgroup
\begingroup\color{TrickPurple}
\textbf{Step 3 --- ALGEBRA: expansion with the imaginary unit.}

\[
\begin{aligned}
\left(\cos(\theta)+\mathrm i\sin(\theta)\right)^2
&=(\cos(\theta))^2
+2\mathrm i\cos(\theta)\sin(\theta)
+\mathrm i^2(\sin(\theta))^2\\
&=(\cos(\theta))^2-(\sin(\theta))^2
+\mathrm i\left(2\cos(\theta)\sin(\theta)\right).
\end{aligned}
\]

\endgroup
\begingroup\color{TrickPurple}
\textbf{Step 4 --- TRICK: comparison of real and imaginary parts.} Euler's formula also
gives \(\exp(2\mathrm i\theta)=\cos(2\theta)+\mathrm i\sin(2\theta)\).
Equal complex numbers have equal real parts and equal imaginary parts:

\[
\cos(2\theta)=(\cos(\theta))^2-(\sin(\theta))^2,
\qquad
\sin(2\theta)=2\cos(\theta)\sin(\theta).
\]

\endgroup
\textbf{Step 5 --- CHECK.} At \(\theta=0\), the cosine formula gives \(1=1-0\) and the
sine formula gives \(0=2(1)(0)\). This checks one angle. The preceding exact
identities establish the general result.

\textbf{When it helps.} Converting trigonometric expressions into exponentials can
make angle combinations easier to manipulate. The real and imaginary parts then recover the corresponding real identities.

\section{T-002 Taylor expansions}

\textbf{Objects and conditions.} \(x\) is an independent real scalar and \(a\) is a fixed
real expansion center. \(f(x)\) is a real scalar-valued function. \(N\) is a fixed
nonnegative integer polynomial degree. \(k\) is an integer summation index;
\(t\) is an auxiliary real scalar input used when writing derivatives before
evaluation. Both \(f(t)\) and \(f(x)\) are values of the same function at their
displayed inputs. The factorial is \(k!=1\cdot2\cdots k\) for \(k\ge1\), with \(0!=1\).

\textbf{TRICK --- Taylor polynomial.} The fixed scalar coefficients are

\[
c_0=f(a),\qquad
c_k=\frac1{k!}\left.\frac{d^k}{dt^k}\left(f(t)\right)\right|_{t=a},
\qquad 1\leq k\leq N.
\]

Then the scalar polynomial is

\[
P_N(x;a)=\sum_{k=0}^{N}c_k(x-a)^k.
\]

For \(N=3\), the full expression is

\[
\begin{aligned}
P_3(x;a)={}&f(a)
+\left.\frac{d}{dt}\left(f(t)\right)\right|_{t=a}(x-a)\\
&+\frac1{2!}\left.\frac{d^2}{dt^2}\left(f(t)\right)\right|_{t=a}(x-a)^2\\
&+\frac1{3!}\left.\frac{d^3}{dt^3}\left(f(t)\right)\right|_{t=a}(x-a)^3.
\end{aligned}
\]

A Maclaurin expansion is the special case \(a=0\). The superscript on a derivative
counts repeated differentiation; it does not square or cube the derivative.

\subsection{The error is part of the tool}

If \(f\) has continuous derivatives through order \(N+1\) on an interval containing
\(a\) and \(x\), Taylor's theorem gives a scalar remainder \(R_N(x;a)\):

\[
f(x)=P_N(x;a)+R_N(x;a),
\]

\[
R_N(x;a)=\frac1{(N+1)!}
\left.\frac{d^{N+1}}{dt^{N+1}}\left(f(t)\right)\right|_{t=\xi}
(x-a)^{N+1},
\]

for some real scalar \(\xi\) between \(a\) and \(x\) when they differ. If a fixed
nonnegative real scalar \(M\) bounds the absolute value of that derivative
throughout the intervening interval, then

\[
|R_N(x;a)|\leq\frac{M|x-a|^{N+1}}{(N+1)!}.
\]

An infinite Taylor series equals \(f(x)\) only where the remainder tends to zero
as \(N\) increases. Smoothness alone does not guarantee that equality. A finite truncation is written with \(\approx\) when its remainder is omitted.
See \href{https://openstax.org/books/calculus-volume-2/pages/6-3-taylor-and-maclaurin-series}{OpenStax, Calculus Volume 2, §6.3}.

\subsection{Common infinite expansions and their valid inputs}

Here \(x\) is an independent real scalar, and \(k\) is an integer summation index.
Each displayed function has a real scalar output. For every real \(x\),

\[
\begin{aligned}
\exp(x)&=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{x^k}{k!}
=1+x+\frac{x^2}{2!}+\frac{x^3}{3!}+\cdots,\\
\sin(x)&=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^k x^{2k+1}}{(2k+1)!}
=x-\frac{x^3}{3!}+\frac{x^5}{5!}-\cdots,\\
\cos(x)&=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^k x^{2k}}{(2k)!}
=1-\frac{x^2}{2!}+\frac{x^4}{4!}-\cdots.
\end{aligned}
\]

The sine and cosine inputs are angles in radians. The summation formulas
specify every term; the trailing dots only illustrate their pattern. These
series converge to their displayed functions at every real input. Keeping
only finitely many terms requires an approximation sign and an error bound.

The geometric expansion has a narrower range of valid inputs:

\[
\frac{1}{1-x}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}x^k
=1+x+x^2+x^3+\cdots,\qquad |x|<1.
\]

Although the function \(1/(1-x)\) exists at \(x=2\), this series does not converge
there. The convergence interval is therefore part of the representation. See \href{https://openstax.org/books/calculus-volume-2/pages/6-4-working-with-taylor-series}{OpenStax, Calculus Volume 2, §6.4}.

Two further expansions occur repeatedly in the nonlinear and parameter-integral
problems. For a real scalar \(x\) with \(|x|<1\),

\[
\ln(1+x)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n+1}x^n}{n},
\qquad
\arctan(x)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^n x^{2n+1}}{2n+1}.
\]

Here \(n\) is an integer summation index. Both series converge uniformly on every
closed interval \(|x|\le r<1\). Consequently, their first terms give
\(\ln(1+x)=x+O(x^2)\) and \(\arctan(x)=x+O(x^3)\) as \(x\to0\). These local estimates
are useful even when the full infinite series is not needed.

\subsection{Worked use: approximate a sine with a stated error}

\textbf{Step 1 --- SETUP.} \(f(x)=\sin(x)\) is a real scalar function, with real scalar
angle \(x\) in radians. The expansion has center \(a=0\) and order \(N=4\). The target input is
\(x=1/10\) radians. There are no varying parameters.

\textbf{Step 2 --- CALCULUS: the required derivatives.}

\[
\begin{aligned}
f(t)&=\sin(t),\\
\frac{d}{dt}\left(f(t)\right)&=\cos(t),\\
\frac{d^2}{dt^2}\left(f(t)\right)&=-\sin(t),\\
\frac{d^3}{dt^3}\left(f(t)\right)&=-\cos(t),\\
\frac{d^4}{dt^4}\left(f(t)\right)&=\sin(t),\\
\frac{d^5}{dt^5}\left(f(t)\right)&=\cos(t).
\end{aligned}
\]

At \(t=0\), the value and first four derivatives are respectively \(0,1,0,-1,0\).
The derivative formulas are listed in the methods reference; here their values
at zero supply the expansion coefficients.

\begingroup\color{TrickPurple}
\textbf{Step 3 --- ALGEBRA: the Taylor polynomial.}

\[
\begin{aligned}
P_4(x;0)
&=0+1(x-0)+\frac0{2!}(x-0)^2
+\frac{-1}{3!}(x-0)^3+\frac0{4!}(x-0)^4\\
&=x-\frac{x^3}{1\cdot2\cdot3}
=x-\frac{x^3}{6}.
\end{aligned}
\]

Although this polynomial's highest nonzero power is three, it is also the
fourth-order Taylor polynomial because its fourth-order coefficient is zero.

\endgroup
\begingroup\color{TrickPurple}
\textbf{Step 4 --- APPROXIMATION: the fifth-derivative remainder bound.} Since
\(|\cos(t)|\leq1\) for real \(t\), the fixed scalar bound \(M=1\) is valid:

\[
\sin(x)=x-\frac{x^3}{6}+R_4(x;0),
\qquad |R_4(x;0)|\leq\frac{|x|^5}{5!}.
\]

\endgroup
\begingroup\color{TrickPurple}
\textbf{Step 5 --- ALGEBRA: exact evaluation of the polynomial.}

\[
\begin{aligned}
P_4(1/10;0)
&=\frac1{10}-\frac{(1/10)^3}{6}\\
&=\frac1{10}-\frac1{1000\cdot6}\\
&=\frac{600}{6000}-\frac1{6000}
=\frac{599}{6000}.
\end{aligned}
\]

\endgroup
\begingroup\color{TrickPurple}
\textbf{Step 6 --- APPROXIMATION: the approximation and its error bound.}

\[
\sin(1/10)\approx\frac{599}{6000},
\qquad
\left|\sin(1/10)-\frac{599}{6000}\right|
\leq\frac1{10^5\cdot120}
=\frac1{12\,000\,000}.
\]

This bound applies to the exact rational approximation shown. A rounded decimal
value introduces an additional error that is separate from the Taylor remainder.

\endgroup
\textbf{Step 7 --- CHECK.} The correction \(x^3/6\) is positive at \(x=1/10\), so the
polynomial estimate is slightly smaller than \(x\). Also, both the polynomial and
the actual sine are zero at the expansion center. These plausibility checks
supplement the theorem's error bound.

\textbf{When it helps.} An expansion is particularly useful near an input where the
function and its derivatives are easy to evaluate. The remainder estimate
determines how far the resulting polynomial can be trusted.

\section{T-003 Binomial expansion}

\textbf{Objects and conditions.} \(r,s\) are real or complex scalar expressions.
\(n\) is a fixed nonnegative integer, and \(k\) is an integer index from zero to \(n\).
The binomial coefficient is the scalar integer

\[
\binom nk=\frac{n!}{k!(n-k)!}.
\]

\textbf{TRICK --- exact finite identity.}

\[
(r+s)^n=\sum_{k=0}^n\binom nk r^{n-k}s^k.
\]

For a zero power, the corresponding factor is \(1\); the endpoint terms are
\(r^n\) and \(s^n\). This is a finite polynomial identity. A fractional exponent
requires a different, generally infinite expansion with convergence conditions.

\subsection{Worked use: expand a shifted cubic}

\textbf{Step 1 --- SETUP.} \(x\) is an independent real scalar. \(h\) is a real scalar
increment. The choices \(r=x\), \(s=h\), and \(n=3\) give the cubic expansion.

\begingroup\color{TrickPurple}
\textbf{Step 2 --- ALGEBRA: the binomial coefficients.}

\[
\begin{aligned}
\binom30&=\frac{3!}{0!3!}=1,\\
\binom31&=\frac{3!}{1!2!}=\frac{6}{1\cdot2}=3,\\
\binom32&=\frac{3!}{2!1!}=\frac{6}{2\cdot1}=3,\\
\binom33&=\frac{3!}{3!0!}=1.
\end{aligned}
\]

\endgroup
\begingroup\color{TrickPurple}
\textbf{Step 3 --- TRICK: the full expansion.}

\[
\begin{aligned}
(x+h)^3
&=1(x^3)+3(x^2h)+3(xh^2)+1(h^3)\\
&=x^3+3x^2h+3xh^2+h^3.
\end{aligned}
\]

\endgroup
\textbf{Step 4 --- CHECK.} At \(h=0\) the expression reduces to \(x^3\); at \(x=0\) it
reduces to \(h^3\). These checks do not replace the binomial identity but can expose
a missing endpoint term. For a square, the same tool gives
\((x+h)^2=x^2+2xh+h^2\).

\textbf{When it helps.} Shifted inputs occur in derivative definitions and series
calculations. The coefficients prevent expansion errors without hiding terms.

\section{T-004 Completing the square}

\textbf{Objects and conditions.} \(x\) is an independent real scalar; \(a,b,c\) are fixed
real scalar coefficients, with \(a\ne0\). The scalar expression is \(ax^2+bx+c\).

\textbf{TRICK --- exact identity.}

\[
ax^2+bx+c=a\left(x+\frac{b}{2a}\right)^2+c-\frac{b^2}{4a}.
\]

\subsection{Worked use: expose a quadratic's shape}

\textbf{Step 1 --- SETUP.} The real scalar function is \(q(x)=2x^2-8x+11\) for all
real \(x\). The coefficients \(2,-8,11\) are fixed.

\begingroup\color{TrickPurple}
\textbf{Step 2 --- ALGEBRA: the quadratic coefficient.}

\[
q(x)=2(x^2-4x)+11.
\]

\endgroup
\begingroup\color{TrickPurple}
\textbf{Step 3 --- TRICK: completion of the square.} Since
\((x-2)^2=x^2-4x+4\),

\[
x^2-4x=(x-2)^2-4.
\]

\endgroup
\begingroup\color{TrickPurple}
\textbf{Step 4 --- ALGEBRA: substitution and simplification.}

\[
\begin{aligned}
q(x)&=2\left((x-2)^2-4\right)+11\\
&=2(x-2)^2-8+11\\
&=2(x-2)^2+3.
\end{aligned}
\]

\endgroup
\textbf{Step 5 --- CHECK: expansion of the result.}

\[
2(x-2)^2+3=2(x^2-4x+4)+3
=2x^2-8x+8+3=2x^2-8x+11.
\]

The square is nonnegative, so \(q(x)\geq3\), with equality at \(x=2\). The exact
rewrite exposes that feature without requiring an optimization calculation.

\textbf{When it helps.} A shifted-square denominator or root may reveal the right
identity, domain, or later integration method. The resulting form can suggest a substitution or make a sign restriction apparent.

\section{T-005 Rationalization}

\textbf{Objects and conditions.} If \(r,s\) are real scalar expressions, the exact
difference-of-squares identity is

\[
(r-s)(r+s)=r^2-s^2.
\]

The factor \(r+s\) is often called the conjugate of \(r-s\) in this real-root
algebra context. Multiplying a fraction by \((r+s)/(r+s)\) preserves it only when
\(r+s\ne0\).

\subsection{Worked use: remove a root difference}

\textbf{Step 1 --- SETUP.} \(x\) is an independent real scalar with \(x>-1\) and \(x\ne0\).
The real scalar function under consideration is

\[
f(x)=\frac{\sqrt{1+x}-1}{x}.
\]

The square root is the nonnegative real root. On this domain,
\(\sqrt{1+x}+1>0\), so the conjugate multiplier is not zero.

\begingroup\color{TrickPurple}
\textbf{Step 2 --- TRICK: the conjugate multiplier.}

\[
f(x)=\frac{\sqrt{1+x}-1}{x}
\frac{\sqrt{1+x}+1}{\sqrt{1+x}+1}.
\]

\endgroup
\begingroup\color{TrickPurple}
\textbf{Step 3 --- ALGEBRA: cancellation on the stated domain.}

\[
\begin{aligned}
f(x)&=\frac{(\sqrt{1+x}-1)(\sqrt{1+x}+1)}{x(\sqrt{1+x}+1)}\\
&=\frac{(\sqrt{1+x})^2-1^2}{x(\sqrt{1+x}+1)}\\
&=\frac{1+x-1}{x(\sqrt{1+x}+1)}\\
&=\frac{x}{x(\sqrt{1+x}+1)}\\
&=\frac1{\sqrt{1+x}+1},\qquad x>-1,\quad x\ne0.
\end{aligned}
\]

\endgroup
\textbf{Step 4 --- CHECK.} At the allowed input \(x=3\), the original expression gives
\((\sqrt4-1)/3=(2-1)/3=1/3\); the rewritten expression gives \(1/(\sqrt4+1)=1/3\).
The original function remains undefined at \(x=0\). The rewritten expression has
a value there and supplies a continuous extension, not a new value of the original
function. See D1-004 for the same domain distinction.

\textbf{When it helps.} A root difference may be hard to simplify or numerically
evaluate near cancellation. Rationalization preserves exact equality where its
divisions are allowed. It can prepare an expression for a limit calculation.

\section{T-006 Logarithm identities}

\textbf{Objects and conditions.} \(r,s\) are positive real scalar expressions and \(p\)
is a fixed real scalar exponent. The natural logarithm \(\ln(r)\) is real. On
these real domains,

\[
\ln(rs)=\ln(r)+\ln(s),\qquad
\ln\left(\frac r s\right)=\ln(r)-\ln(s),\qquad
\ln(r^p)=p\ln(r).
\]

These identities need positivity in this real formulation. Complex logarithms
require branch choices and do not permit these unrestricted rewrites. See the
exponential and logarithm definitions in \href{https://dlmf.nist.gov/4.2}{NIST DLMF §4.2}.

\subsection{Worked use: expose a product and power inside a logarithm}

\textbf{Step 1 --- SETUP.} \(x>0\) is an independent real scalar. The real scalar
function is

\[
q(x)=\ln\left(\frac{(x^2+1)^3}{x^2}\right).
\]

On this domain, \(x^2+1>0\) and \(x^2>0\), so both logarithms introduced below
have positive arguments.

\begingroup\color{TrickPurple}
\textbf{Step 2 --- TRICK: the logarithm of a quotient.}

\[
q(x)=\ln\left((x^2+1)^3\right)-\ln(x^2).
\]

\endgroup
\begingroup\color{TrickPurple}
\textbf{Step 3 --- TRICK: the logarithm power identity.}

\[
q(x)=3\ln(x^2+1)-2\ln(x),\qquad x>0.
\]

\endgroup
\textbf{Step 4 --- CHECK.} At \(x=1\), the original expression is
\(\ln((1+1)^3/1)=\ln(8)\). The new expression is \(3\ln(2)-2\ln(1)=3\ln(2)\),
which equals \(\ln(8)\) by the power identity, with \(\ln(1)=0\).

\textbf{When it helps.} Revealing sums and fixed multipliers can organize a later
derivative calculation. This algebraic rewrite does not eliminate the dependence
of \(x^2+1\) on \(x\).

For a version declared on \(x\ne0\), including negative inputs, the last expression
would instead be \(3\ln(x^2+1)-2\ln(|x|)\). The absolute value keeps the real
logarithm's input positive. The expression \(\ln(x)\) alone would exclude every
negative input.

\section{T-007 Hyperbolic identities and real branches}

For a real scalar \(u\), the scalar functions are defined by

\[
\cosh(u)=\frac{\exp(u)+\exp(-u)}{2},\qquad
\sinh(u)=\frac{\exp(u)-\exp(-u)}{2}.
\]

Expansion of the squares and cancellation of their common terms give

\[
\cosh^2(u)-\sinh^2(u)=1,\qquad
\cosh^2(u)+\sinh^2(u)=1+2\sinh^2(u),\qquad
\cosh^2(u)=\frac{1+\cosh(2u)}{2}.
\]

The first identity converts a difference of squares into a hyperboloid equation;
the second and third simplify its surface-area factor. These are supporting
identities. The coordinate substitution and the integral rule remain calculus
operations in the main chapters.

\subsection{Worked use: a square root with its sign accounted for}

The independent real scalar \(t\) and the real coordinate \(u\) are related by
\(t=\sinh(u)\). Thus

\[
\sqrt{1+t^2}=\sqrt{1+\sinh^2(u)}
=\sqrt{\cosh^2(u)}=|\cosh(u)|=\cosh(u).
\]

The final equality is valid because \(\cosh(u)\ge1\) for real \(u\). In general,
\(\sqrt{h^2}=|h|\) for a real scalar \(h\), and replacement by \(h\) alone is justified
only when \(h\ge0\). The real inverse

\[
u=\operatorname{arsinh}(t)=\ln\left(t+\sqrt{1+t^2}\right)
\]

exists for every real \(t\): \(\sinh(u)\) is strictly increasing, and the logarithm's
argument is positive even when \(t<0\). By contrast, \(\cosh(u)\) is even and is
not one-to-one on all real inputs. Its nonnegative inverse branch
\(\operatorname{arcosh}(t)=\ln(t+\sqrt{t^2-1})\) is defined for \(t\ge1\).
The expressions with inverse hyperbolic functions used in I3-006 and I3-009
refer to these real conventions.

\section{Returning to the calculation}

A useful identity changes the representation without losing the original
conditions. An approximation also introduces an error that becomes part of the
answer. In the worked problems, the supporting calculation is followed by a
return to the derivative, integral, or limit for which it was needed. This makes
it possible to see both why the tool was chosen and what it accomplished.
\endgroup

\section{D1-001 --- Polynomial differentiation by linearity}
\label{problem:D1-001}
\markright{D1-001}
{\small Hints: page~\pageref{hints:D1-001}.}\par\medskip

\textbf{Objects and dependencies.} \(x\in\mathbb R\) is the independent scalar variable.
The scalar function is \(f(x)=4x^3-5x^2+7x-9\), defined for every real \(x\).
All coefficients are fixed real scalars. Find the scalar-valued function

\[
\frac{d}{dx}\left(f(x)\right).
\]
\subsection*{Worked solution}

\textbf{Step 1 --- SETUP.} \(x\in\mathbb R\) is independent and scalar; \(f(x)=4x^3-5x^2+7x-9\)
is scalar. The coefficients do not vary with \(x\). A polynomial is differentiable
at every real input.

\begin{ChoiceStep}
\textbf{Step 2 --- CHOICE.} This is a sum of constant multiples of powers of \(x\).
Linearity and the power rule apply directly; no product or chain-rule decomposition is required.

\end{ChoiceStep}
\textbf{Step 3 --- CALCULUS: termwise differentiation.}

\[
\begin{aligned}
\frac{d}{dx}\left(f(x)\right)
&=\frac{d}{dx}\left(4x^3-5x^2+7x-9\right)\\
&=4\frac{d}{dx}\left(x^3\right)
-5\frac{d}{dx}\left(x^2\right)
+7\frac{d}{dx}\left(x\right)
-\frac{d}{dx}\left(9\right)\\
&=4(3x^{3-1})-5(2x^{2-1})+7(1)-0.
\end{aligned}
\]

\begingroup\color{AlgebraBlue}
\textbf{Step 4 --- ALGEBRA: exponents and coefficients.}

\[
\begin{aligned}
3-1&=2,\qquad 2-1=1,\\
4(3x^2)-5(2x)+7(1)-0
&=(4\cdot3)x^2-(5\cdot2)x+7\\
&=12x^2-10x+7.
\end{aligned}
\]

\endgroup
\textbf{Result.}

\[
\boxed{\frac{d}{dx}\left(f(x)\right)=12x^2-10x+7,
\qquad x\in\mathbb R.}
\]

\textbf{Step 5 --- CHECK: comparison with the definition at one input.} At \(x=0\), the
formula gives \(12(0)^2-10(0)+7=7\). For a nonzero scalar increment \(h\),
\(f(h)=4h^3-5h^2+7h-9\) and \(f(0)=-9\).

\begingroup\color{AlgebraBlue}
\textbf{Step 6 --- ALGEBRA: the difference quotient.}

\[
\begin{aligned}
\frac{f(h)-f(0)}{h}
&=\frac{4h^3-5h^2+7h-9-(-9)}{h}\\
&=\frac{4h^3-5h^2+7h}{h}\\
&=\frac{h(4h^2-5h+7)}{h}\\
&=4h^2-5h+7,\qquad h\ne0.
\end{aligned}
\]

\endgroup
\textbf{Step 7 --- CHECK: the limit.} As \(h\to0\), the expression tends to \(7\),
matching the derivative at zero. This checks one input; it is not an independent
proof of the formula at every input.

\textbf{Discussion.} Linearity preserves each signed coefficient.
Constants and constant multiples are distinguished before rule application.

\section{D1-002 --- Radicals and negative powers}
\label{problem:D1-002}
\markright{D1-002}
{\small Hints: page~\pageref{hints:D1-002}.}\par\medskip

\textbf{Objects and dependencies.} \(x\) is an independent real scalar with \(x>0\).
The scalar function is

\[
f(x)=5\sqrt{x}-\frac{3}{x^2}+2.
\]

The square root is the nonnegative real square root. All coefficients are fixed
scalars. Find the derivative with respect to \(x\) and evaluate it at \(x=4\).
\subsection*{Worked solution}

\textbf{Step 1 --- SETUP.} \(x>0\) is the independent real scalar.
\(f(x)=5\sqrt{x}-3/x^2+2\) is scalar, with fixed coefficients. The positive domain
both defines the root smoothly and excludes division by zero.

\begin{ChoiceStep}
\textbf{Step 2 --- CHOICE.} Rewriting the root and reciprocal as powers makes the power
rule applicable to both nonconstant terms.

\end{ChoiceStep}
\begingroup\color{AlgebraBlue}
\textbf{Step 3 --- ALGEBRA: an equivalent expression on the original domain.}

\[
\sqrt{x}=x^{1/2},\qquad \frac{1}{x^2}=x^{-2},\qquad
f(x)=5x^{1/2}-3x^{-2}+2,\qquad x>0.
\]

\endgroup
\textbf{Step 4 --- CALCULUS: the unsimplified derivative.}

\[
\begin{aligned}
\frac{d}{dx}\left(f(x)\right)
&=5\frac{d}{dx}\left(x^{1/2}\right)
-3\frac{d}{dx}\left(x^{-2}\right)
+\frac{d}{dx}\left(2\right)\\
&=5\left(\frac12 x^{1/2-1}\right)
-3\left((-2)x^{-2-1}\right)+0.
\end{aligned}
\]

\begingroup\color{AlgebraBlue}
\textbf{Step 5 --- ALGEBRA: signs and powers.}

\[
\begin{aligned}
\frac12-1&=\frac12-\frac22=-\frac12,\\
-2-1&=-3,\qquad (-3)(-2)=6,\\
5\left(\frac12 x^{-1/2}\right)-3\left((-2)x^{-3}\right)+0
&=\frac52 x^{-1/2}+6x^{-3}\\
&=\frac{5}{2\sqrt{x}}+\frac{6}{x^3}.
\end{aligned}
\]

\endgroup
\textbf{Step 6 --- CALCULUS: evaluation of the derivative.}

\[
\left.\frac{d}{dx}\left(f(x)\right)\right|_{x=4}
=\frac{5}{2\sqrt{4}}+\frac{6}{4^3}.
\]

\begingroup\color{AlgebraBlue}
\textbf{Step 7 --- ALGEBRA: a common denominator.}

\[
\begin{aligned}
\sqrt4&=2,\qquad 4^3=4\cdot4\cdot4=64,\\
\frac{5}{2(2)}+\frac6{64}
&=\frac54+\frac3{32}\\
&=\frac{5\cdot8}{4\cdot8}+\frac3{32}\\
&=\frac{40+3}{32}=\frac{43}{32}.
\end{aligned}
\]

\endgroup
\textbf{Result.} The derivative is \(5/(2\sqrt{x})+6/x^3\) for \(x>0\), and its value at
\(x=4\) is the scalar number \(43/32\).

\textbf{Step 8 --- CHECK: sign and domain.} Both derivative terms are positive for
\(x>0\). This agrees with the behavior of the original terms: \(5\sqrt{x}\) increases,
and \(-3/x^2\) becomes less negative as \(x\) increases. The derivative formula also
retains the restriction \(x>0\). This sign check catches a likely sign mistake; it
does not establish the exact magnitude by itself.

\textbf{Discussion.} A power representation unifies the radical and reciprocal terms.
Subtracting a term whose power-rule coefficient is negative produces a positive
term; write the multiplication to see why.

\section{D1-003 --- Differentiation with fixed parameters}
\label{problem:D1-003}
\markright{D1-003}
{\small Hints: page~\pageref{hints:D1-003}.}\par\medskip

\textbf{Objects and dependencies.} \(x\in\mathbb R\) is the independent scalar variable.
\(a,b\in\mathbb R\) are fixed scalar parameters. The scalar output is

\[
f(x;a,b)=ax^2+bx+4.
\]

Differentiate with respect to \(x\), holding \(a\) and \(b\) fixed. Then evaluate the
derivative at \(x=3\) with parameter values \(a=2\) and \(b=-1\).
\subsection*{Worked solution}

\textbf{Step 1 --- SETUP.} \(x\in\mathbb R\) is the independent scalar.
\(a,b\in\mathbb R\) are fixed scalar parameters. The output \(f(x;a,b)=ax^2+bx+4\)
is scalar. Its derivative with respect to \(x\) is taken with both parameters fixed.

\begin{ChoiceStep}
\textbf{Step 2 --- CHOICE.} The constant-multiple rule applies because \(a,b\) are independent of \(x\).
Symbolic parameter values can be retained throughout the differentiation.

\end{ChoiceStep}
\textbf{Step 3 --- CALCULUS.} Holding \(a\) and \(b\) fixed,

\[
\begin{aligned}
\frac{d}{dx}\left(f(x;a,b)\right)
&=a\frac{d}{dx}\left(x^2\right)
+b\frac{d}{dx}\left(x\right)
+\frac{d}{dx}\left(4\right)\\
&=a(2x)+b(1)+0.
\end{aligned}
\]

\begingroup\color{AlgebraBlue}
\textbf{Step 4 --- ALGEBRA.}

\[
a(2x)+b(1)+0=2ax+b.
\]

\endgroup
\textbf{Step 5 --- CALCULUS: evaluation at the specified input and parameters.}

\[
\left.\frac{d}{dx}\left(f(x;a,b)\right)
\right|_{x=3,\,a=2,\,b=-1}
=2(2)(3)+(-1).
\]

\begingroup\color{AlgebraBlue}
\textbf{Step 6 --- ALGEBRA.}

\[
2(2)(3)+(-1)=4\cdot3-1=12-1=11.
\]

\endgroup
\textbf{Result.} The derivative is \(2ax+b\), with \(a,b\) fixed, for all real \(x\).
The requested evaluated slope is the scalar number \(11\).

\textbf{Step 7 --- CHECK: differentiation with the parameters fixed.} First define
the scalar function \(g(x)=f(x;2,-1)=2x^2-x+4\). Its derivative is

\[
\frac{d}{dx}\left(g(x)\right)=2(2x)-1+0=4x-1.
\]

The coefficient simplification is \textbf{ALGEBRA}. At \(x=3\), \(4(3)-1=12-1=11\),
matching the parameterized calculation. This consistency check applies because
the parameters really are fixed. If \(a\) were replaced by a function \(a(x)\), the
dependencies and required rules would change.

\textbf{Discussion.} “Constant” is a relationship to the variable of
differentiation, not a requirement that the symbol be a numeral.

\section{D1-004 --- Cancellation and a removable singularity}
\label{problem:D1-004}
\markright{D1-004}
{\small Hints: page~\pageref{hints:D1-004}.}\par\medskip

\textbf{Objects and dependencies.} \(x\) is an independent real scalar with \(x\ne3\).
The scalar function is

\[
f(x)=\frac{x^2-9}{x-3}.
\]

Find its derivative on its domain. A student cancels a factor and concludes that
this original function has derivative \(1\) at \(x=3\) too. Explain whether that
conclusion follows.
\subsection*{Worked solution}

\textbf{Step 1 --- SETUP.} \(x\ne3\) is the independent real scalar and
\(f(x)=(x^2-9)/(x-3)\) is scalar. The original definition excludes \(x=3\).

\begin{ChoiceStep}
\textbf{Step 2 --- CHOICE.} The difference-of-squares factorization permits cancellation on the stated
domain, reducing the derivative calculation to a linear function.

\end{ChoiceStep}
\begingroup\color{TrickPurple}
\textbf{Step 3 --- TRICK: difference of squares.} For scalar expressions \(r,s\),
the identity from T-005 is

\[
r^2-s^2=(r-s)(r+s).
\]

With \(r=x\) and \(s=3\),

\[
x^2-9=x^2-3^2=(x-3)(x+3).
\]

\endgroup
\begingroup\color{AlgebraBlue}
\textbf{Step 4 --- ALGEBRA: cancellation on the original domain.}

\[
\begin{aligned}
f(x)&=\frac{(x-3)(x+3)}{x-3}\\
&=\frac{x-3}{x-3}(x+3)\\
&=1(x+3)=x+3,\qquad x\ne3.
\end{aligned}
\]

\endgroup
\textbf{Step 5 --- CALCULUS: derivative of the equivalent local formula.} At each
allowed input \(x\ne3\), the function agrees with \(x+3\) in a neighborhood:

\[
\frac{d}{dx}\left(f(x)\right)
=\frac{d}{dx}\left(x+3\right)
=\frac{d}{dx}\left(x\right)+\frac{d}{dx}\left(3\right)
=1+0=1.
\]

The last addition is \textbf{ALGEBRA}.

\textbf{Result.} The derivative is \(1\) for \(x\ne3\). The original function has no
derivative at \(x=3\) because it has no value there. Defining a new scalar function
\(\widetilde f(x)=x+3\) for every real \(x\) defines a continuous extension at the excluded point. This extension has a
derivative at \(3\), but it is a different function with a different domain.

\textbf{Step 6 --- CHECK: the difference quotient on its domain.} For an allowed
input \(x\ne3\), take a nonzero scalar increment \(h\) small enough that \(x+h\ne3\).

\begingroup\color{AlgebraBlue}
\textbf{Step 7 --- ALGEBRA.}

\[
\frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h}
=\frac{(x+h+3)-(x+3)}{h}
=\frac{x+h+3-x-3}{h}
=\frac{h}{h}=1.
\]

\endgroup
\begin{DomainStep}
\textbf{Step 8 --- CHECK AND DOMAIN.} The quotient's limit is \(1\) at every allowed input.
This verifies the derivative on the original domain and supplies no derivative
at the excluded input. The student's claim confuses the function with its extension.

\end{DomainStep}
\textbf{Discussion.} Cancellation preserves the excluded point in the original domain.
The reduced expression and its continuous extension represent different functions.

\section{D1-005 --- Difference-quotient derivation}
\label{problem:D1-005}
\markright{D1-005}
{\small Hints: page~\pageref{hints:D1-005}.}\par\medskip

\textbf{Objects and dependencies.} \(x\in\mathbb R\) is the independent scalar variable.
The scalar function is \(f(x)=x^2+2x\). Use the limit definition to calculate its
derivative, without using the power rule as the derivation. Then evaluate the
derivative at \(x=-1\).

During the calculation, \(h\in\mathbb R\) is a nonzero scalar increment that tends
to zero; it is not a new input of \(f\).
\subsection*{Worked solution}

\textbf{Step 1 --- SETUP.} \(x\in\mathbb R\) is independent, \(f(x)=x^2+2x\) is scalar,
and \(h\ne0\) is a real scalar increment tending to zero. All inputs \(x+h\) are
allowed because the function is defined on all of \(\mathbb R\).

\begin{ChoiceStep}
\textbf{Step 2 --- CHOICE.} The difference quotient is reduced algebraically for nonzero increments
before its limit is taken. This ordering prevents division by zero.

\end{ChoiceStep}
\textbf{Step 3 --- CALCULUS: the derivative definition.}

\[
\frac{d}{dx}\left(f(x)\right)
=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h}.
\]

\begingroup\color{TrickPurple}
\textbf{Step 4 --- TRICK: the shifted square.} The expansion is
T-003. The scalar identity
\((r+s)^2=r^2+2rs+s^2\), with \(r=x\) and \(s=h\), gives

\[
f(x+h)=(x+h)^2+2(x+h)=x^2+2xh+h^2+2x+2h.
\]

The distribution \(2(x+h)=2x+2h\) is an \textbf{ALGEBRA} step.

\endgroup
\begingroup\color{AlgebraBlue}
\textbf{Step 5 --- ALGEBRA: subtraction of the original value.}

\[
\begin{aligned}
f(x+h)-f(x)
&=(x^2+2xh+h^2+2x+2h)-(x^2+2x)\\
&=x^2+2xh+h^2+2x+2h-x^2-2x\\
&=(x^2-x^2)+(2x-2x)+2xh+h^2+2h\\
&=2xh+h^2+2h\\
&=h(2x+h+2).
\end{aligned}
\]

\endgroup
\begingroup\color{AlgebraBlue}
\textbf{Step 6 --- ALGEBRA: division for a nonzero increment.}

\[
\frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h}
=\frac{h(2x+h+2)}{h}
=2x+h+2,\qquad h\ne0.
\]

\endgroup
\textbf{Step 7 --- CALCULUS: the limiting derivative and its value.} In the
limit, \(x\) stays fixed and \(h\to0\):

\[
\frac{d}{dx}\left(f(x)\right)=\lim_{h\to0}(2x+h+2)=2x+2.
\]

\[
\left.\frac{d}{dx}\left(f(x)\right)\right|_{x=-1}=2(-1)+2.
\]

\begingroup\color{AlgebraBlue}
\textbf{Step 8 --- ALGEBRA.}

\[
2(-1)+2=-2+2=0.
\]

\endgroup
\textbf{Result.} The derivative is \(2x+2\) for every real \(x\); the slope at \(x=-1\)
is the scalar number \(0\). The value \(f(-1)=1-2=-1\) is a function value, a
different quantity from its slope.

\textbf{Step 9 --- CHECK: comparison with the power rule.}

\[
\frac{d}{dx}\left(x^2+2x\right)
=\frac{d}{dx}\left(x^2\right)+2\frac{d}{dx}\left(x\right)
=2x+2(1)=2x+2.
\]

The coefficient simplification is \textbf{ALGEBRA}. This agrees with the definition
calculation. Replacing \(x\) by \(-1\) before differentiating would give the constant
expression \(-1\), whose zero derivative does not determine the derivative function
or justify evaluation of its slope. The agreement at this particular input is coincidental and does not validate
that order of operations.

\textbf{Discussion.} Parentheses preserve the subtraction of the complete
reference value in the difference quotient. Increment cancellation is valid
only before the limiting operation, while the increment is nonzero.

\section{D1-006 --- Algebraic reduction before differentiation}
\label{problem:D1-006}
\markright{D1-006}
{\small Hints: page~\pageref{hints:D1-006}.}\par\medskip

\textbf{Objects and dependencies.} \(x\) is an independent real scalar with \(x\ne0\).
The scalar function is

\[
f(x)=\frac{2x^3-3x+4}{x}.
\]

Find the scalar-valued derivative and the scalar slope at \(x=2\).
Choose and explain your method before calculating.
\subsection*{Worked solution}

\textbf{Step 1 --- SETUP.} \(x\ne0\) is the independent real scalar;
\(f(x)=(2x^3-3x+4)/x\) is scalar. The requested slope at \(x=2\) is valid because
\(2\ne0\). There are no varying parameters.

\begin{ChoiceStep}
\textbf{Step 2 --- CHOICE.} The monomial denominator permits termwise division on \(x\ne0\).
The reduced sum of powers can be differentiated by linearity, avoiding a
quotient-rule expansion while retaining the original exclusion.

\end{ChoiceStep}
\begingroup\color{AlgebraBlue}
\textbf{Step 3 --- ALGEBRA: termwise division on the original domain.}

\[
\begin{aligned}
f(x)&=\frac{2x^3}{x}-\frac{3x}{x}+\frac4x\\
&=2x^{3-1}-3+4x^{-1}\\
&=2x^2-3+4x^{-1},\qquad x\ne0.
\end{aligned}
\]

\endgroup
\textbf{Step 4 --- CALCULUS: derivative of the rewritten function.}

\[
\begin{aligned}
\frac{d}{dx}\left(f(x)\right)
&=2\frac{d}{dx}\left(x^2\right)
-\frac{d}{dx}\left(3\right)
+4\frac{d}{dx}\left(x^{-1}\right)\\
&=2(2x)-0+4((-1)x^{-1-1}).
\end{aligned}
\]

\begingroup\color{AlgebraBlue}
\textbf{Step 5 --- ALGEBRA.}

\[
\begin{aligned}
-1-1&=-2,\\
2(2x)-0+4((-1)x^{-2})
&=4x-4x^{-2}=4x-\frac4{x^2}.
\end{aligned}
\]

\endgroup
\textbf{Step 6 --- CALCULUS: evaluation at the specified input.}

\[
\left.\frac{d}{dx}\left(f(x)\right)\right|_{x=2}
=4(2)-\frac4{2^2}.
\]

\begingroup\color{AlgebraBlue}
\textbf{Step 7 --- ALGEBRA.}

\[
4(2)-\frac4{2^2}=8-\frac4{4}=8-1=7.
\]

\endgroup
\textbf{Result.} The derivative is \(4x-4/x^2\) for \(x\ne0\), and the slope at \(2\)
is the scalar number \(7\).

\textbf{Step 8 --- CHECK: the slope from a difference quotient.} Declare \(h\)
as a nonzero scalar increment tending to zero, small enough that \(2+h\ne0\).
Using the equivalent formula, \(f(2)=2(2)^2-3+4/2=8-3+2=7\).

\begingroup\color{TrickPurple}
\textbf{Step 9 --- TRICK: the shifted-square expansion.} See
T-003.

\[
(2+h)^2=2^2+2(2)h+h^2=4+4h+h^2.
\]

\endgroup
\begingroup\color{AlgebraBlue}
\textbf{Step 10 --- ALGEBRA: simplification of the quotient.}

\[
\begin{aligned}
f(2+h)&=2(4+4h+h^2)-3+\frac4{2+h}\\
&=8+8h+2h^2-3+\frac4{2+h}\\
&=5+8h+2h^2+\frac4{2+h},\\
f(2+h)-f(2)&=5+8h+2h^2+\frac4{2+h}-7\\
&=8h+2h^2+\frac4{2+h}-2\\
&=8h+2h^2+\frac{4-2(2+h)}{2+h}\\
&=8h+2h^2+\frac{4-4-2h}{2+h}\\
&=8h+2h^2-\frac{2h}{2+h},\\
\frac{f(2+h)-f(2)}h
&=\frac{h(8+2h-2/(2+h))}{h}\\
&=8+2h-\frac2{2+h},\qquad h\ne0.
\end{aligned}
\]

\endgroup
\textbf{Step 11 --- CHECK: the limit.}

\[
\lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(2+h)-f(2)}h
=8+0-\frac22=8-1=7.
\]

The last arithmetic is \textbf{ALGEBRA}. This independently verifies the requested
slope at \(2\), while the differentiation rules supply the general formula.

\textbf{Discussion.} Algebraic reduction can expose a sum of powers inside a rational
expression. Rule selection follows the reduced structure and its preserved domain.

\section{D2-001 --- Differentiate a composition}
\label{problem:D2-001}
\markright{D2-001}
{\small Hints: page~\pageref{hints:D2-001}.}\par\medskip

\textbf{Objects and dependencies.} \(x\in\mathbb R\) is an independent scalar variable. The scalar-valued function \(f:\mathbb R\to\mathbb R\) is

\[
f(x)=(3x^2+1)^4.
\]

There are no other variables or parameters. Find the scalar-valued derivative

\[
\frac{d}{dx}\left(f(x)\right).
\]
\subsection*{Worked solution}

\textbf{Step 1 --- SETUP.} The independent input \(x\) is scalar. The output \(f(x)=(3x^2+1)^4\) is scalar and is defined for all real \(x\). Introduce scalar-valued functions

\[
g(x)=3x^2+1,\qquad h(u)=u^4,\qquad f(x)=h(g(x)).
\]

Here \(u\) is an independent scalar input when defining and differentiating \(h(u)\). In the composition, set \(u=g(x)\), so the inner value depends on \(x\).

\begin{ChoiceStep}
\textbf{Step 2 --- CHOICE.} The fourth power is a composition with the nonconstant inner function \(g(x)\). The chain rule supplies the outer derivative evaluated at \(g(x)\) and the derivative of \(g(x)\).

\end{ChoiceStep}
\textbf{Step 3 --- CALCULUS: the outer derivative.} The power rule gives

\[
\frac{d}{du}\left(h(u)\right)
=\frac{d}{du}\left(u^4\right)
=4u^3.
\]

\textbf{Step 4 --- CALCULUS: the inner derivative.} Linearity and the power rule give the inner derivative:

\[
\begin{aligned}
\frac{d}{dx}\left(g(x)\right)
&=\frac{d}{dx}\left(3x^2+1\right)\\
&=3\frac{d}{dx}\left(x^2\right)+\frac{d}{dx}\left(1\right)\\
&=3(2x)+0.
\end{aligned}
\]

\begingroup\color{AlgebraBlue}
\textbf{Step 5 --- ALGEBRA.} Multiplication of the numerical factors and removal of the zero term give:

\[
3(2x)+0=(3\cdot2)x+0=6x+0=6x.
\]

\endgroup
\textbf{Step 6 --- CALCULUS: the complete chain-rule expression.}

\[
\begin{aligned}
\frac{d}{dx}\left(f(x)\right)
&=\left.\frac{d}{du}\left(h(u)\right)\right|_{u=g(x)}
\frac{d}{dx}\left(g(x)\right)\\
&=\left.\left(4u^3\right)\right|_{u=3x^2+1}(6x)\\
&=4(3x^2+1)^3(6x).
\end{aligned}
\]

\begingroup\color{AlgebraBlue}
\textbf{Step 7 --- ALGEBRA.} All factors are scalar, so they may be reordered:

\[
4(3x^2+1)^3(6x)=(4\cdot6)x(3x^2+1)^3=24x(3x^2+1)^3.
\]

\endgroup
\textbf{Result.}

\[
\boxed{\frac{d}{dx}\left(f(x)\right)=24x(3x^2+1)^3.}
\]

\textbf{Step 8 --- CHECK.} At \(x=0\), the formula gives \(24(0)(1)^3=0\). Independently, the derivative definition at zero is

\[
\lim_{\varepsilon\to0}\frac{f(\varepsilon)-f(0)}{\varepsilon}.
\]

Here \(\varepsilon\) is a nonzero scalar increment tending to zero. For the check, the binomial identity
\((a+b)^4=a^4+4a^3b+6a^2b^2+4ab^3+b^4\), with \(a=1\), \(b=3\varepsilon^2\), gives the following \textbf{IDENTITY and ALGEBRA} steps:

\[
\begin{aligned}
f(\varepsilon)&=1+4(3\varepsilon^2)+6(3\varepsilon^2)^2+4(3\varepsilon^2)^3+(3\varepsilon^2)^4\\
&=1+12\varepsilon^2+54\varepsilon^4+108\varepsilon^6+81\varepsilon^8,\\
f(0)&=1,\\
\frac{f(\varepsilon)-f(0)}{\varepsilon}
&=\frac{12\varepsilon^2+54\varepsilon^4+108\varepsilon^6+81\varepsilon^8}{\varepsilon}\\
&=12\varepsilon+54\varepsilon^3+108\varepsilon^5+81\varepsilon^7.
\end{aligned}
\]

Each term tends to zero, so the limit is zero. This checks the answer at one input; it is not a separate proof of the derivative formula at every input.

\textbf{Discussion.} A composition is differentiated with respect to the outer function's argument before evaluation at the inner function. The chain-rule factor depends on the declared functional dependence, not the apparent complexity of the expression.

\label{problem:D2-002}
\markright{D2-002}
{\small Hints: page~\pageref{hints:D2-002}.}\par\medskip

\textbf{Objects and dependencies.} \(x\) is an independent real scalar angle in radians.
The real scalar function \(f(x)=x^2\sin(x)\) is defined for all real \(x\). No parameters
vary. Find \(\frac{d}{dx}\left(f(x)\right)\) and its value at \(x=\pi\), where \(\pi\) is
the fixed circle constant.
\subsection*{Worked solution}

\textbf{Step 1 --- SETUP.} \(x\) is an independent real scalar angle in radians.
\(f(x)=x^2\sin(x)\) is real scalar-valued. Let \(u(x)=x^2\) and \(v(x)=\sin(x)\);
both are real scalar functions on all real inputs. The output derivative is scalar.

\begin{ChoiceStep}
\textbf{Step 2 --- CHOICE.} Both factors depend on \(x\); the product rule is therefore applicable. Differentiating
both and multiplying would omit the product rule's two terms.

\end{ChoiceStep}
\textbf{Step 3 --- CALCULUS: the product derivative.}

\[
\frac{d}{dx}\left(u(x)\right)=2x,\qquad
\frac{d}{dx}\left(v(x)\right)=\cos(x),
\]

\[
\frac{d}{dx}\left(f(x)\right)
=(2x)\sin(x)+x^2\cos(x).
\]

\begingroup\color{AlgebraBlue}
\textbf{Step 4 --- ALGEBRA: evaluation of the derivative.} Since \(\sin(\pi)=0\) and
\(\cos(\pi)=-1\),

\[
\left.\frac{d}{dx}\left(f(x)\right)\right|_{x=\pi}
=(2\pi)(0)+\pi^2(-1)=0-\pi^2=-\pi^2.
\]

\endgroup
\begingroup\color{TrickPurple}
\textbf{Step 5 --- TRICK: an angle-addition identity.} For real scalar angles
\(a,b\) in radians,

\[
\sin(a+b)=\sin(a)\cos(b)+\cos(a)\sin(b).
\]

Taking \(a=\pi\) and \(b=h\), with real scalar increment \(h\), gives
\(\sin(\pi+h)=0\cos(h)+(-1)\sin(h)=-\sin(h)\).
This identity follows from the Euler identities.

\endgroup
\textbf{Step 6 --- CHECK.} The difference quotient at this input, with real scalar
increment \(h\ne0\), is

\[
\frac{f(\pi+h)-f(\pi)}h
=(\pi+h)^2\frac{\sin(\pi+h)}h.
\]

Using the identity from Step 5, the limit as
\(h\to0\) is \(\pi^2(-1)=-\pi^2\), using \(\lim_{h\to0}\sin(h)/h=1\) in radians.
This independently verifies the requested evaluated derivative.

\section{D2-003 --- A quotient with an excluded input}
\label{problem:D2-003}
\markright{D2-003}
{\small Hints: page~\pageref{hints:D2-003}.}\par\medskip

\textbf{Objects and dependencies.} \(x\) is an independent real scalar with \(x\ne1\).
The real scalar function is \(f(x)=(x^2+1)/(x-1)\). Find the scalar-valued derivative
\(\frac{d}{dx}\left(f(x)\right)\) and its value at \(x=2\).
\subsection*{Worked solution}

\textbf{Step 1 --- SETUP.} \(x\ne1\) is an independent real scalar and
\(f(x)=(x^2+1)/(x-1)\) is real scalar-valued. Let \(u(x)=x^2+1\) and \(v(x)=x-1\).
All coefficients are fixed. The derivative is scalar-valued on this domain.

\begin{ChoiceStep}
\textbf{Step 2 --- CHOICE.} The nonconstant denominator does not cancel against the numerator. The quotient rule applies on the domain excluding its zero.

\end{ChoiceStep}
\textbf{Step 3 --- CALCULUS: the quotient-rule numerator.}

\[
\frac{d}{dx}\left(u(x)\right)=2x,\qquad
\frac{d}{dx}\left(v(x)\right)=1,
\]

\[
\frac{d}{dx}\left(f(x)\right)
=\frac{(2x)(x-1)-(x^2+1)(1)}{(x-1)^2}.
\]

\begingroup\color{AlgebraBlue}
\textbf{Step 4 --- ALGEBRA: expansion of the numerator.}

\[
(2x)(x-1)-(x^2+1)(1)
=2x^2-2x-x^2-1=(2-1)x^2-2x-1=x^2-2x-1.
\]

Therefore

\[
\frac{d}{dx}\left(f(x)\right)=\frac{x^2-2x-1}{(x-1)^2},\qquad x\ne1.
\]

At \(x=2\), the scalar value is

\[
\frac{2^2-2(2)-1}{(2-1)^2}=
\frac{4-4-1}{1^2}=\frac{-1}{1}=-1.
\]

\endgroup
\textbf{Step 5 --- CHECK: derivative of an equivalent expression.} Since
\((x-1)(x+1)+2=x^2-1+2=x^2+1\),

\[
f(x)=x+1+\frac{2}{x-1},\qquad x\ne1.
\]

The chain and sum rules give

\[
\frac{d}{dx}\left(f(x)\right)=1-\frac{2}{(x-1)^2}
=\frac{(x-1)^2-2}{(x-1)^2}
=\frac{x^2-2x+1-2}{(x-1)^2}.
\]

This matches the result. The excluded input remains excluded.

\textbf{Step 2 --- CHOICE.} The logarithm rule is applied to \(t\), then evaluated at

\end{ChoiceStep}
\textbf{Step 3 --- CALCULUS: the two dependent layers.}

\frac{d}{dt}\left(h(t)\right)=\frac1t,\qquad

\(f(x)\ge\ln(1)=0=f(0)\). Do not replace \(\ln(1+x^2)\) by \(\ln(1)+\ln(x^2)\):

the chain rule implies

\[
2f(x)\frac{d}{dx}\left(f(x)\right)=2x.
\]

Dividing by \(2f(x)>0\) gives \(x/f(x)=x/\sqrt{1+x^2}\), which agrees.

\textbf{Step 2 --- CHOICE.} The nested composition has three derivative factors: the linear
argument, the sine function, and the outer cubic power. Their dependencies
are represented separately before the factors are assembled.

\end{ChoiceStep}
\textbf{Step 3 --- CALCULUS: derivative of the inner composition.}

\[
\frac{d}{dx}\left(g(x)\right)=2,\qquad
\frac{d}{dt}\left(h(t)\right)=\cos(t),
\]

=\left.\cos(t)\right|_{t=2x}(2)=2\cos(2x).

\[
\frac{d}{ds}\left(p(s)\right)=3s^2,
\]

\[
\frac{d}{dx}\left(f(x)\right)
=\left.3s^2\right|_{s=\sin(2x)}\left(2\cos(2x)\right).
\]

\[
3(\sin(2x))^2(2\cos(2x))
=(3\cdot2)(\sin(2x))^2\cos(2x)
=6(\sin(2x))^2\cos(2x).
\]

At \(x=\pi/4\), \(2x=2(\pi/4)=\pi/2\), \(\sin(\pi/2)=1\), and

At \(a=\pi/2\) and \(b=2h\), for real scalar increment \(h\),

\[
\sin(\pi/2+2h)=1\cos(2h)+0\sin(2h)=\cos(2h).
\]

This is another use of the Euler identities.

\endgroup

\(f(\pi/4+h)=(\sin(\pi/2+2h))^3=(\cos(2h))^3\).
This expression is even in \(h\) and differentiable,
so its derivative at \(h=0\) is zero. This checks the evaluated slope; the chain-rule
calculation establishes the general formula.

\textbf{Step 2 --- CHOICE.} The first integral has an odd integrand on a symmetric interval.
The absolute-value integrand instead requires a piecewise representation,
with the interval partitioned at its sign change.

\end{ChoiceStep}
\textbf{Step 3 --- CALCULUS: the signed integral.}

\[
\int_{-1}^{1}q(x)\,dx
=\left.\frac{x^2}{2}\right|_{x=-1}^{x=1}
=\frac{1^2}{2}-\frac{(-1)^2}{2}.
\]

\begingroup\color{AlgebraBlue}
\textbf{Step 4 --- ALGEBRA: cancellation.}

\[
\frac{1^2}{2}-\frac{(-1)^2}{2}=\frac12-\frac12=0.
\]

\endgroup
\textbf{Step 5 --- CALCULUS: partition of the nonnegative integral.}

\[
\begin{aligned}
\int_{-1}^{1}|q(x)|\,dx
&=\int_{-1}^{0}(-x)\,dx+\int_0^1 x\,dx\\
&=\left.-\frac{x^2}{2}\right|_{x=-1}^{x=0}
+\left.\frac{x^2}{2}\right|_{x=0}^{x=1}.
\end{aligned}
\]

\begingroup\color{AlgebraBlue}
\textbf{Step 6 --- ALGEBRA: endpoint subtraction on each interval.}

\[
\left(-\frac{0^2}{2}-\left(-\frac{(-1)^2}{2}\right)\right)
+\left(\frac{1^2}{2}-\frac{0^2}{2}\right)
=\left(0+\frac12\right)+\left(\frac12-0\right)=1.
\]

\endgroup
\textbf{Step 7 --- CHECK.} The absolute-value graph consists of two right triangles,
each of base one and height one. Their total area is
\(2(1\cdot1/2)=1\). The signed integral is zero because the negative triangle
and positive triangle have opposite signed contributions.

\section{I2-001 --- Evaluate a definite integral}
\label{problem:I2-001}
\markright{I2-001}
{\small Hints: page~\pageref{hints:I2-001}.}\par\medskip

\textbf{Objects and dependencies.} \(x\) is a scalar integration variable in \([0,1]\). Define the scalar-valued function \(q(x)=2x(x^2+1)^3\). All quantities are real. Integrate with respect to \(x\), with lower limit \(x=0\) and upper limit \(x=1\). Find the scalar number

\[
\int_{0}^{1} q(x)\,dx
=\int_{0}^{1} 2x(x^2+1)^3\,dx.
\]
\subsection*{Worked solution}

\textbf{Step 1 --- SETUP.} The original scalar variable is \(x\in[0,1]\), and the scalar integrand is \(q(x)=2x(x^2+1)^3\). Introduce

\[
g(x)=x^2+1,\qquad u=g(x),\qquad h(u)=u^3.
\]

Both \(g\) and \(h\) are scalar-valued functions. The new coordinate \(u\) depends on \(x\) until we rewrite the integral in terms of \(u\). Polynomials have the continuity and differentiability needed for the substitution theorem.

\begin{ChoiceStep}
\textbf{Step 2 --- CHOICE.} The integrand contains a power of \(x^2+1\) and the factor \(2x\), which is the derivative of \(x^2+1\). This derivative match identifies the direct substitution theorem.

\end{ChoiceStep}
\textbf{Step 3 --- CALCULUS.}

\[
\begin{aligned}
\frac{d}{dx}\left(g(x)\right)
&=\frac{d}{dx}\left(x^2+1\right)\\
&=\frac{d}{dx}\left(x^2\right)+\frac{d}{dx}\left(1\right)\\
&=2x+0=2x.
\end{aligned}
\]

The last removal of \(+0\) is an \textbf{ALGEBRA} simplification.

\begingroup\color{AlgebraBlue}
\textbf{Step 4 --- ALGEBRA: the required derivative factor.} Since the factors are scalars,

\[
2x(x^2+1)^3=(x^2+1)^3(2x)
=h(g(x))\frac{d}{dx}\left(g(x)\right).
\]

\endgroup
\begingroup\color{AlgebraBlue}
\textbf{Step 5 --- ALGEBRA: the transformed bounds.}

\[
\begin{aligned}
x=0&\quad\Longrightarrow\quad u=g(0)=0^2+1=0+1=1,\\
x=1&\quad\Longrightarrow\quad u=g(1)=1^2+1=1+1=2.
\end{aligned}
\]

\endgroup
\textbf{Step 6 --- CALCULUS: the substitution theorem.}

\[
\begin{aligned}
\int_{0}^{1} 2x(x^2+1)^3\,dx
&=\int_{0}^{1}
\left(h(g(x))\frac{d}{dx}\left(g(x)\right)\right)\,dx\\
&=\int_{g(0)}^{g(1)} h(u)\,du\\
&=\int_{1}^{2} u^3\,du.
\end{aligned}
\]

The whole integral changes coordinates, including the integration variable and both bounds. The derivative factor is accounted for by the theorem.

\textbf{Step 7 --- CALCULUS: an antiderivative.} The power rule for integration is

\[
\int u^p\,du=\frac{u^{p+1}}{p+1}+C,\qquad p\ne-1,
\]

on an interval where the powers and this rule are defined. Here \(p=3\) is a fixed scalar exponent and \(u\in[1,2]\). Choose one antiderivative \(H(u)\):

\[
H(u)=\frac{u^{3+1}}{3+1}=\frac{u^4}{4}.
\]

The exponent and denominator simplifications are \textbf{ALGEBRA}. A definite integral needs one antiderivative; any added constant cancels in the endpoint difference.

\textbf{Step 8 --- CALCULUS: endpoint evaluation.}

\[
\int_{1}^{2} u^3\,du=H(2)-H(1)=\frac{2^4}{4}-\frac{1^4}{4}.
\]

\begingroup\color{AlgebraBlue}
\textbf{Step 9 --- ALGEBRA.}

\[
\begin{aligned}
2^4&=2\cdot2\cdot2\cdot2=16,\\
1^4&=1\cdot1\cdot1\cdot1=1,\\
\frac{2^4}{4}-\frac{1^4}{4}
&=\frac{16}{4}-\frac{1}{4}
=\frac{16-1}{4}
=\frac{15}{4}.
\end{aligned}
\]

\endgroup
\textbf{Result.}

\[
\boxed{\int_{0}^{1} 2x(x^2+1)^3\,dx=\frac{15}{4}.}
\]

\textbf{Step 10 --- CHECK: derivative in the original variable.} The candidate antiderivative is the scalar function \(Q(x)=(x^2+1)^4/4\). The constant-multiple and chain rules give

\[
\frac{d}{dx}\left(Q(x)\right)
=\frac14\,4(x^2+1)^3(2x).
\]

\textbf{ALGEBRA:} \((1/4)\cdot4=1\), so this becomes \(2x(x^2+1)^3=q(x)\), the original integrand. Also,

\[
Q(1)-Q(0)=\frac{(1^2+1)^4}{4}-\frac{(0^2+1)^4}{4}
=\frac{2^4}{4}-\frac{1^4}{4}=\frac{15}{4}.
\]

\textbf{Discussion.} A derivative factor identifies a candidate coordinate substitution. A proportional match requires an explicit constant multiplier; an inverse-coordinate substitution additionally requires a valid branch.

\section{I2-002 --- A derivative match needing a fixed multiplier}
\label{problem:I2-002}
\markright{I2-002}
{\small Hints: page~\pageref{hints:I2-002}.}\par\medskip

\textbf{Objects and dependencies.} \(x\) is a real scalar integration variable;
\(q(x)=x(1+x^2)^4\) is real scalar-valued on all real inputs. Find the scalar
antiderivative family \(\int q(x)\,dx\).
\subsection*{Worked solution}

\textbf{Step 1 --- SETUP.} \(x\) is the real scalar integration variable and
\(q(x)=x(1+x^2)^4\) is real scalar-valued. Let \(g(x)=1+x^2\) and
\(h(u)=u^4\), with independent real scalar input \(u\) when defining \(h(u)\).
Before substitution \(u=g(x)\) is dependent on \(x\). All real \(x\) are allowed.

\begin{ChoiceStep}
\textbf{Step 2 --- CHOICE.} The factor \(x\) is one half of the inner derivative.
The compensating multiplier is retained outside the transformed integral.

\end{ChoiceStep}
\textbf{Step 3 --- CALCULUS: the inner derivative.}

\[
\frac{d}{dx}\left(g(x)\right)=\frac{d}{dx}\left(1+x^2\right)=0+2x=2x.
\]

\begingroup\color{AlgebraBlue}
\textbf{Step 4 --- ALGEBRA: the transformed factors.}

\[
x(1+x^2)^4=\frac12(2x)(1+x^2)^4
=\frac12h(g(x))\frac{d}{dx}\left(g(x)\right).
\]

No division by \(x\) was used; the identity holds at \(x=0\) too.

\endgroup
\textbf{Step 5 --- CALCULUS: integration in the new variable.}

\[
\int q(x)\,dx=\frac12\int u^4\,du
=\frac12\frac{u^{4+1}}{4+1}+C.
\]

\begingroup\color{AlgebraBlue}
\textbf{Step 6 --- ALGEBRA: return to the original variable.}

\[
4+1=5,\qquad \frac12\frac{u^5}{5}=\frac{u^5}{2\cdot5}=\frac{u^5}{10},
\]

\[
\int q(x)\,dx=\frac{(1+x^2)^5}{10}+C.
\]

The real scalar constant \(C\) is independent of \(x\).

\endgroup
\textbf{Step 7 --- CHECK.} By the chain rule,

\[
\frac{d}{dx}\left(\frac{(1+x^2)^5}{10}+C\right)
=\frac1{10}5(1+x^2)^4(2x)+0.
\]

The fixed coefficient is \((1/10)\cdot5\cdot2=10/10=1\), so the derivative
is \(x(1+x^2)^4=q(x)\).

\section{I2-003 --- A cubic inside an exponential}
\label{problem:I2-003}
\markright{I2-003}
{\small Hints: page~\pageref{hints:I2-003}.}\par\medskip

\textbf{Objects and dependencies.} \(x\) is a real scalar integration variable from
\(x=0\) to \(x=1\). The real scalar function is \(q(x)=3x^2\exp(x^3)\).
Find the real scalar number \(\int_0^1q(x)\,dx\).
\subsection*{Worked solution}

\textbf{Step 1 --- SETUP.} \(x\) is the real scalar integration variable from zero to one.
\(q(x)=3x^2\exp(x^3)\) has real scalar output. Let \(g(x)=x^3\) and
\(h(u)=\exp(u)\), with auxiliary independent real scalar input \(u\).
All functions used are smooth on the interval; the result is a number.

\begin{ChoiceStep}
\textbf{Step 2 --- CHOICE.} The numerator factor \(3x^2\) is exactly the derivative
of the exponential's inner input.

\end{ChoiceStep}
\textbf{Step 3 --- CALCULUS: the coordinate derivative and bounds.}

\[
\frac{d}{dx}\left(g(x)\right)=3x^2,\qquad
u=g(0)=0^3=0,\qquad u=g(1)=1^3=1.
\]

\textbf{Step 4 --- CALCULUS: the transformed integral.}

\[
\int_0^1q(x)\,dx
=\int_0^1h(g(x))\frac{d}{dx}\left(g(x)\right)\,dx
=\int_0^1\exp(u)\,du.
\]

The numerical bounds happen to agree, but the variable and its meaning changed.

\textbf{Step 5 --- CALCULUS: evaluation of the primitive.}

\[
\int_0^1\exp(u)\,du=\exp(1)-\exp(0).
\]

\begingroup\color{AlgebraBlue}
\textbf{Step 6 --- ALGEBRA: the constant value.} Since \(\exp(0)=1\), the
answer is \(\exp(1)-1\). The notation \(e=\exp(1)\) denotes a fixed real constant.

\endgroup
\textbf{Step 7 --- CHECK.} The proposed antiderivative is the real scalar function \(F(x)=\exp(x^3)\).
Its derivative is \(\exp(x^3)(3x^2)=q(x)\). Also
\(F(1)-F(0)=\exp(1)-1\). This verifies both the integrand match and endpoint result.

\label{problem:I2-004}
\markright{I2-004}
{\small Hints: page~\pageref{hints:I2-004}.}\par\medskip

\textbf{Objects and dependencies.} \(x\) is a real scalar angle in radians and is the
integration variable. Define \(q(x)=\cos(x)/(2+\sin(x))\) with real scalar output.
The denominator is at least one, so every real input is allowed. Find
\(\int q(x)\,dx\).
\subsection*{Worked solution}

\textbf{Step 1 --- SETUP.} \(x\) is a real scalar integration variable in radians.
\(q(x)=\cos(x)/(2+\sin(x))\) is real scalar-valued for all real inputs.
Let \(g(x)=2+\sin(x)\) and \(h(u)=1/u\) for independent real scalar \(u>0\).
The actual inner values satisfy \(1\le g(x)\le3\).

\begin{ChoiceStep}
\textbf{Step 2 --- CHOICE.} The denominator's derivative appears in the numerator.
Use a reciprocal antiderivative after the change of variable.

\end{ChoiceStep}
\textbf{Step 3 --- CALCULUS: the inner derivative.}

\[
\frac{d}{dx}\left(g(x)\right)=0+\cos(x)=\cos(x).
\]

\textbf{Step 4 --- CALCULUS: integration in the new variable.}

\[
\int q(x)\,dx=\int\frac1u\,du=\ln(u)+C,\qquad u>0.
\]

The scalar constant \(C\) is independent of the original input \(x\).

\begingroup\color{AlgebraBlue}
\textbf{Step 5 --- ALGEBRA: return to the original variable.}

\[
\int q(x)\,dx=\ln(2+\sin(x))+C.
\]

No absolute value is needed because the logarithm's input is always positive.

\endgroup
\textbf{Step 6 --- CHECK: derivative of the complete answer.}

\[
\frac{d}{dx}\left(\ln(2+\sin(x))+C\right)
=\frac1{2+\sin(x)}(\cos(x))+0
=\frac{\cos(x)}{2+\sin(x)}=q(x).
\]

The substitution theorem does not require \(g(x)\) to be one-to-one. A vanishing
inner derivative at some inputs does not invalidate this antiderivative.

\item Equal logarithmic divergences cancel only under the specified principal-value prescription.
\end{enumerate}
\subsection*{I3-006 --- Near a line source: differentiation under an integral}
\label{hints:I3-006}
Problem and worked solution: page~\pageref{problem:I3-006}.

\begin{enumerate}
\item The scalar substitution \(s=\rho\sinh u\) removes the square root for \(\rho>0\).
\item Its endpoints depend on \(\rho\); the resulting closed form is twice an inverse hyperbolic sine.
\item Under differentiation with respect to \(\rho\), the kernel becomes \(-\rho(\rho^2+s^2)^{-3/2}\).
\end{enumerate}
\subsection*{I3-007 --- A weakly singular CEM panel integral}
\label{hints:I3-007}
Problem and worked solution: page~\pageref{problem:I3-007}.

\begin{enumerate}
\item On the triangle, \(v=y/x\) runs from zero to one for \(x>0\).
\item The absolute determinant of the coordinate Jacobian is \(u\).
\item One power of \(u\) cancels the \(1/R\) singularity. A \(1/R^2\) kernel retains a logarithmically divergent \(u\) integral.
\end{enumerate}
\subsection*{I3-008 --- An ellipsoidal weighted volume}
\label{hints:I3-008}
Problem and worked solution: page~\pageref{problem:I3-008}.

\begin{enumerate}
\item Scaling by the three semiaxes maps the ellipsoid to the unit ball.
\item Spherical coordinates on that ball contribute a further factor \(r^2\sin\theta\).
\item Integration by parts reduces \(\int_0^1r^2\exp(-r^2)\,dr\) to a boundary term and an error-function integral.
\end{enumerate}
\subsection*{I3-009 --- Finite and infinite surface area of a hyperboloid}
\label{hints:I3-009}
Problem and worked solution: page~\pageref{problem:I3-009}.

\begin{enumerate}
\item The surface area measure is the length of the tangent cross product.
\item The identity \(\cosh^2u=1+\sinh^2u\) makes \(t=\sinh u\) useful.
\item The area becomes an integral of \(\sqrt{1+2t^2}\) on a finite symmetric interval, whose size grows without bound as \(U\) increases.
\end{enumerate}
\subsection*{D1-001 --- Polynomial differentiation by linearity}
\label{hints:D1-001}
Problem and worked solution: page~\pageref{problem:D1-001}.

\begin{enumerate}
\item Identify the four terms and keep the signs attached to them.
\item The fixed coefficients can be taken outside their derivative operators; the constant term has derivative zero.
\item Differentiate \(x^3\), \(x^2\), and \(x\) separately, then multiply their coefficients and simplify their exponents.
\end{enumerate}
\subsection*{D1-002 --- Radicals and negative powers}
\label{hints:D1-002}
Problem and worked solution: page~\pageref{problem:D1-002}.

\begin{enumerate}
\item Rewrite both the root and the reciprocal as powers of the same independent variable.
\item On \(x>0\), use \(\sqrt{x}=x^{1/2}\) and \(1/x^2=x^{-2}\).
\item The derivative of the negative reciprocal term has a positive coefficient because \((-3)(-2)=6\). Evaluate at \(x=4\) after differentiating.
\end{enumerate}
\subsection*{D1-003 --- Differentiation with fixed parameters}
\label{hints:D1-003}
Problem and worked solution: page~\pageref{problem:D1-003}.

\begin{enumerate}
\item The symbols \(a\) and \(b\) are fixed with respect to \(x\); they are not functions \(a(x)\) and \(b(x)\).
\item The constant-multiple rule applies to the fixed parameters \(a,b\) in the same way as to numerical coefficients.
\item Obtain the derivative with its parameters visible, then substitute \(x=3\), \(a=2\), and \(b=-1\).
\end{enumerate}
\subsection*{D1-004 --- Cancellation and a removable singularity}
\label{hints:D1-004}
Problem and worked solution: page~\pageref{problem:D1-004}.

\begin{enumerate}
\item Can the numerator be factored using a difference-of-squares identity?
\item Write \(x^2-9=(x-3)(x+3)\). Cancellation is allowed only when \(x-3\ne0\).
\item The simplified formula describes the original function only on \(x\ne3\). A formula that fills in the missing value defines a different function.
\end{enumerate}
\subsection*{D1-005 --- Difference-quotient derivation}
\label{hints:D1-005}
Problem and worked solution: page~\pageref{problem:D1-005}.

\begin{enumerate}
\item Write \(f(x+h)\) using the same function definition, replacing every input \(x\) by \(x+h\).
\item Expand the square, then subtract all of \(f(x)\) inside parentheses.
\item Factor a single \(h\) from the numerator, divide for \(h\ne0\), and only then take the limit.
\end{enumerate}
\subsection*{D1-006 --- Algebraic reduction before differentiation}
\label{hints:D1-006}
Problem and worked solution: page~\pageref{problem:D1-006}.

\begin{enumerate}
\item Inspect the denominator: it is a single power of the independent variable.
\item Divide each numerator term by \(x\) separately while preserving the condition \(x\ne0\).
\item The resulting expression is a sum of powers. Calculate the derivative, then evaluate its value at \(x=2\).
\end{enumerate}
\subsection*{D2-001 --- Differentiate a composition}
\label{hints:D2-001}
Problem and worked solution: page~\pageref{problem:D2-001}.

\begin{enumerate}
\item Which expression is raised to the fourth power?
\item Define the inner function \(g(x)=3x^2+1\), and the outer function \(h(u)=u^4\). Then \(f(x)=h(g(x))\).
\item Differentiate \(h(u)\) with respect to \(u\), evaluate at \(u=g(x)\), and multiply by the derivative of \(g(x)\) with respect to \(x\).
\end{enumerate}
\subsection*{D2-002 --- A polynomial times a sine}
\label{hints:D2-002}
Problem and worked solution: page~\pageref{problem:D2-002}.

\begin{enumerate}
\item Both factors change with \(x\); use the product rule.
\item Differentiate \(x^2\) and \(\sin(x)\) separately.
\item Keep the unchanged factor in each product-rule term; then substitute \(x=\pi\).
\end{enumerate}
\subsection*{D2-003 --- A quotient with an excluded input}
\label{hints:D2-003}
Problem and worked solution: page~\pageref{problem:D2-003}.

\begin{enumerate}

\item Use the quotient rule with numerator \(u(x)=x^2+1\) and denominator \(v(x)=x-1\).
\item Expand \(2x(x-1)-(x^2+1)\) before evaluating.
\end{enumerate}
\subsection*{D2-004 --- An exponential of a quadratic}

\end{enumerate}
\subsection*{D2-005 --- Logarithmic composition}
\label{hints:D2-005}

\begin{enumerate}
\item The derivative of a logarithm must include its inner rate of change.
\item Set \(g(x)=1+x^2\) and \(h(t)=\ln(t)\), with \(t>0\).
\item Evaluate \(1/t\) at \(t=g(x)\) and multiply by \(2x\).

\subsection*{D2-006 --- Square-root composition}

\begin{enumerate}

\item Differentiate \(h(t)=t^{1/2}\) on \(t>0\) and \(g(x)=1+x^2\).

\chapter{Literature review and notation decisions}
\markright{}

Reviewed October 5, 2026. Scope: selected introductory calculus textbook sections, a university matrix-calculus course, a matrix-calculus reference, a tensor survey, and continuum-mechanics notes. This is a targeted notation review, not an exhaustive survey or a study demonstrating that one notation improves learning for everyone.

\section{Conventions adopted in this book}

The book uses Leibniz derivative operators with round parentheses and complete
function arguments. Nearby definitions state the object types, dependencies, and
quantities held fixed. These choices preserve familiar mathematical symbols while
making less of their interpretation depend on memory or surrounding context.

The references document established usage and distinctions between mathematical
objects. The choice among compatible conventions is editorial; there is no single
notation that serves every discipline or every reader equally well.

\section{Findings and decisions}

\subsection{R1 --- Ordinary derivatives}

OpenStax explicitly lists the operator form \(\frac{d}{dx}\left(f(x)\right)\) alongside prime and quotient-style notation. It also discusses higher derivatives. The operator form is adopted here. Higher derivatives retain \(d^2/dx^2\), with a nested expansion at their first appearance. Worked calculations use this notation consistently in place of primes and dots. \href{https://openstax.org/books/calculus-volume-1/pages/3-2-the-derivative-as-a-function}{OpenStax, Calculus Volume 1, §3.2}.

\subsection{R2 --- Partial derivatives}

OpenStax defines partial derivatives by changing one coordinate while the other stays fixed, and uses both fraction and subscript notation. The book retains the partial derivative symbol, includes the function arguments, and states nearby which inputs are held fixed. Repeating that information makes each calculation easier to read independently. \href{https://openstax.org/books/calculus-volume-3/pages/4-3-partial-derivatives}{OpenStax, Calculus Volume 3, §4.3}.

\subsection{R3 --- Dependencies and the chain rule}

OpenStax's multivariable chain rule distinguishes independent, intermediate, and dependent variables and uses dependency trees. The dependence \(z(t)=f(x(t),y(t))\) and the evaluation points of the partial derivatives are displayed here. This distinguishes differentiation in an independent coordinate from differentiation along a path. \href{https://openstax.org/books/calculus-volume-3/pages/4-5-the-chain-rule}{OpenStax, Calculus Volume 3, §4.5}.

\subsection{R4 --- Substitution and integrals}

OpenStax uses ordinary integral bounds and differentials, supplies a definite-substitution theorem, and demonstrates changing endpoints. Conventional integral bounds and differentials are retained here. The variable and bounds are described in the accompanying statement, and differential identities are explained when introduced. \href{https://openstax.org/books/calculus-volume-1/pages/5-5-substitution}{OpenStax, Calculus Volume 1, §5.5}.

\subsection{R5 --- Gradients, Jacobians, and derivative maps}

MIT's course treats derivatives as linear operators. For a map from \(n\) inputs to \(m\) outputs, the Jacobian is \(m\times n\). For a scalar output in Euclidean coordinates, the gradient is a column vector and the derivative is represented by its transpose. The gradient, Jacobian, and derivative map are distinguished here by their definitions and shapes. The derivative map is denoted by \(\mathrm D\), with round parentheses around its increment argument. \href{https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/18-s096-matrix-calculus-for-machine-learning-and-beyond-january-iap-2023/pages/lecture-notes-and-readings/}{MIT 18.S096, Lecture Notes and Readings, Lecture 1}.

\subsection{R6 --- Matrix derivatives and constraints}

The Matrix Cookbook defines a scalar function's matrix derivative through derivatives with respect to entries. Its structured-matrix section distinguishes constrained entries from independent entries. Entry derivatives and the shape of the matrix gradient are stated before a compact matrix formula is used. When entries are constrained, the independent parameters are named separately. \href{https://math.uwaterloo.ca/~hwolkowi/matrixcookbook}{Petersen and Pedersen, The Matrix Cookbook, November 15, 2012, §§2 and 2.8}.

\subsection{R7 --- Tensor typography and index meaning}

Kolda and Bader use bold lowercase vectors, bold uppercase matrices, and bold script higher-order tensors, with ordinary scalar component symbols. They distinguish order from rank and note alternative conventions. The same visual distinction is adopted here with a bold calligraphic font. Geometric tensors also have declared spaces and bases, since typography alone does not specify the object. \href{https://www.kolda.net/publication/TensorReview.pdf}{Kolda and Bader, Tensor Decompositions and Applications, SIAM Review 51(3), 2009, §2}.

\subsection{R8 --- Summation shorthand}

Stanford continuum-mechanics notes place explicit sums beside their Einstein-convention equivalents and expand the component equations. Worked solutions retain the summation signs and limits. This makes each contraction visible even to a reader who does not routinely use the Einstein convention. \href{https://biomechanics.stanford.edu/me338/me338_n01.pdf}{Stanford ME338A, Continuum Mechanics, Lecture Notes 01, §1.1.1.1}.

\section{Delimiter and symbol policy}

These are editorial decisions informed by the review:

\begin{itemize}
\item Round parentheses group derivative operands. Square brackets display vectors and matrices. Closed intervals retain their conventional square brackets, with endpoint inequalities spelled out when introduced.
\item Tensor components use declared indices. Square brackets are not a universal tensor marker.
\item Ordinary integrands retain their usual notation. Parentheses group terms when needed to make the expression unambiguous.
\item Evaluation bars mean substitution at specified inputs. The quantities held fixed are described in words, distinguishing that information from evaluation at a point.
\item The inverse sine is \(\arcsin(x)\); the reciprocal of the sine is \(1/\sin(x)\). A square of the sine is written as \((\sin(x))^2\) or the conventional \(\sin^2(x)\).
\item Determinants are written as \(\det(\mathbf A)\), while absolute-value bars denote scalar magnitude.

\end{itemize}
