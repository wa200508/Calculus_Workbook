## I4-001: a difference of exponential kernels

The numerator is $(b-a)x+O(x^2)$ at zero, so division by $x$ has the finite
limit $b-a$. For positive parameters the infinite tail is bounded by a decaying
exponential divided by $x$. Thus both ends converge absolutely.

Independently writing the quotient as $\int_a^b e^{-sx}\,ds$ gives
$J(a,b)=\int_a^b ds/s=\ln(b/a)$. Tonelli applies to the nonnegative
integrand for $b\ge a$; reversing the interval handles $b<a$ with its sign.
The result has the correct antisymmetry and vanishes for equal parameters.
At $a\downarrow0$ it diverges positively. At $a=0$ the original tail is
asymptotic to $1/x$, so the endpoint is genuinely divergent rather than a
removable defect of the logarithmic expression. The text correctly allows
Tonelli to produce an infinite value rather than claiming a finite endpoint
integral. The supplementary checks integrate the canceled original quotient
for ordered, reversed, equal, and close parameters.

**Finding:** the hints, convergence proof, interchange, signs, and result agree
with the independent derivation. No correction required.

## I4-002: a logarithmic kernel

For each $a>-1$, the kernel is continuous after assigning value $a$ at zero.
On a sufficiently small parameter neighborhood, $1+ax$ has a uniform positive
lower bound on $[0,1]$. This justifies
$J'(a)=\int_0^1(1+ax)^{-1}dx=\ln(1+a)/a$, with value one at zero.
For fixed $|a|<1$, the divided logarithm series is dominated termwise by
$|a|^n/n$; its integrated coefficients are therefore
$(-1)^{n+1}a^n/n^2$.

The real integral definition of the dilogarithm is valid for every real
$z<1$. Substitution gives $J(a)=-\operatorname{Li}_2(-a)$ on the entire
stated parameter domain, including positive parameters beyond the local
series radius. At $a=-1$, the singularity at $x=1$ is logarithmic and
integrable; the zero endpoint remains removable. Monotone convergence of
$-J(-r)$ gives the finite value $-\sum_{n\ge1}1/n^2=-\pi^2/6$.
The derivative, however, tends to positive infinity; its endpoint integrand
would be $(1-x)^{-1}$ and is not integrable. The text makes this distinction.

Fresh numerical quadrature uses $x=1-e^{-u}$, which treats the logarithmic
endpoint directly. It verifies the local series, derivative at zero and
nonzero inputs, the endpoint value, and a dilogarithm inversion identity at
a positive parameter outside the series disk.

**Finding:** every sign, coefficient, representation, and endpoint argument
agrees. No correction required.

## I4-003: a damped oscillatory difference

At zero the cosine difference is $(c^2-b^2)x^2/2+O(x^4)$, so the divided
kernel is $O(x)$. Positive damping makes the infinite tail absolutely
integrable, including when one or both frequencies vanish. The derivative
with respect to $c$ is dominated by $e^{-ax}$ and has integral
$c/(a^2+c^2)$. Integrating that derivative from $b$ to $c$ gives
$K=\tfrac12\ln((a^2+c^2)/(a^2+b^2))$, with the integration constant fixed
at equal frequencies.

For positive frequencies, the undamped ordinary improper integral exists.
The text's integration-by-parts tail estimate is correct: a bounded sine
primitive and the decreasing amplitude $e^{-ax}/x$ give a bound $2/(qR)$,
uniformly for $a\ge0$. Applying it separately to both frequencies, together
with finite-interval dominated convergence, justifies the damping limit
$\ln(c/b)$. No absolute domination at the undamped endpoint is asserted.
If exactly one frequency vanishes, the logarithmic limit has the correct
infinite sign; if both vanish the integral is identically zero.

Fresh checks integrate the original damped quotient, including zero and equal
frequencies, and compare a finite undamped cutoff with the stated uniform
tail bound.

**Finding:** convergence, frequency differentiation, and damping removal are
justified, with all exceptional cases consistent. No correction required.

## I4-004: a weighted logarithm

The coordinate $x=\cos\theta$ transforms the integral into
$\int_0^\pi\ln(1+\lambda\cos\theta)\,d\theta$ with both orientation
changes accounted for. On $|\lambda|<1$, local positive denominator bounds
justify differentiation. The tangent half-angle coordinate gives
$\int_0^\pi(1+\lambda\cos\theta)^{-1}d\theta
=\pi/\sqrt{1-\lambda^2}$. Integrating the resulting parameter derivative,
with $J(0)=0$, yields the displayed
$\pi\ln((1+\sqrt{1-\lambda^2})/2)$.

At $\lambda=1$, the angular logarithm has an integrable logarithmic
singularity at $\pi$. The text's lower bound
$(1+\cos\theta)/2\le1+\lambda\cos\theta$, for $1/2\le\lambda\le1$,
is valid for both signs of the cosine and supplies the stated integrable
dominator. Reflection handles $\lambda=-1$. Both endpoint values are
$-\pi\ln2$. The differentiated angular kernel is instead asymptotic to a
nonintegrable inverse square at the singular endpoint, with derivative
limits negative at $1$ and positive at $-1$.

Fresh angular quadrature verifies both the values and interior derivatives;
an endpoint coordinate that resolves the logarithm separately checks
$-\pi\ln2$.

**Finding:** the formula, angular branches, domination, and failed endpoint
interchange all agree. No correction required.

## I4-005: translated-ellipsoid flux

The origin lies inside precisely when $|d|<a$, outside when $|d|>a$, and on
the boundary only at the excluded values $d=\pm a$. The classical divergence
is zero away from the origin. Outside, the divergence theorem therefore
gives zero flux. Inside, excision of a sufficiently small source-centered
ball gives an inner boundary with negative radial orientation; its flux is
$-4\pi$, so the outer ellipsoid flux is $4\pi$.

Thus the limits as $d$ approaches $a$ are $4\pi$ from below and zero from
above. The text correctly says that the smooth-field argument fails at the
excluded boundary-source case. It does not incorrectly infer that every
separately defined improper boundary flux must diverge or assign either
one-sided parameter limit to that case.

Fresh checks integrate the original field over an outward-oriented ellipsoid
parameterization for two interior placements and exterior placements on both
sides of the source.

**Finding:** source placement, inner orientation, flux values, and parameter
limits are correct. No correction required.

## I4-006: near-source panel kernel

I used an independent polar reduction: $0\le\theta\le\pi/4$ and
$0\le r\le\sec\theta$. It gives
$K(h)=\int_0^{\pi/4}(\sqrt{h^2+\sec^2\theta}-h)\,d\theta$.
This independently yields $K(0)=\ln(1+\sqrt2)$, right derivative $-\pi/4$,
and
$0\le K(h)-K(0)+\pi h/4\le h^2/(2\sqrt2)$.

The manuscript's triangle-to-square Jacobian is $u$, and its integration in
$u$ correctly subtracts the positive lower-endpoint root $h$. The subsequent
hyperbolic and tangent substitutions are valid at every fixed $h>0$ and
give an equivalent closed form. In particular,
$\arctan(h/\sqrt{2+h^2})
=\arcsin(h/\sqrt{2(1+h^2)})$, agreeing with the polar evaluation.
Rationalizing the root difference gives the correct nonnegative remainder
and bound, valid for all $h\ge0$.

At positive distances the kernel derivative is locally dominated on the
finite triangle. As $h\downarrow0$, it tends to zero at every nonzero
spatial point, while its integral tends to $-\pi/4$. Consequently there is
no integrable dominator uniform through the source limit, and interchanging
that limit would be wrong. The text explicitly identifies this failure.
Fresh polar quadrature checks the closed form, derivative, remainder
inequality, and one-sided slope.

**Finding:** all transformations, signs, approximation constants, and limit
qualifications are correct. No correction required.

## I4-007: oblique ellipsoidal cap

Scaling by $D=\operatorname{diag}(a,b,c)$ maps the ellipsoid to a unit ball,
with volume factor $abc$ and transformed normal $Dn$. Let
$\rho=\|Dn\|>0$. Rotating that normal to one axis reduces the cap volume
to $\pi abc\int_{h/\rho}^1(1-s^2)\,ds$ in the interior range.
The factored polynomial, full-volume and zero-volume regimes all agree
with this independent calculation.

The derivative is $-\pi abc(1-h^2/\rho^2)/\rho$ for $|h|<\rho$, zero
outside, and zero at both tangent offsets. The quadratic disappearing-cap
behavior proves differentiability at those joins; the nonmatching
one-sided second derivatives correctly preclude twice differentiability.

For a nonunit normal, physical displacement is $dh/\|n\|$, so
$V'(h)=-A_{\rm sec}(h)/\|n\|$. The section area formula retains exactly
this required factor. As a separate check, I parameterized the physical
section by an orthonormal basis of the cutting plane, restricted the
ellipsoid's quadratic form to that plane, completed its square, and
integrated the resulting ellipse areas. This checks the normal factor
without reusing the cap's scaled-ball formula. Fresh numerical checks
include nonunit oblique normals, full and empty caps, interior derivatives,
and both tangent offsets.

**Finding:** geometry, units, endpoint derivatives, and physical section
relations agree. No correction required.

## I4-009: matrix accumulation and perturbation

For symmetric positive-definite $A$, spectral bounds make the square of
$R(s)=(A+sI)^{-1}$ integrable. Differentiating its defining inverse identity
in $s$ gives $R'=-R^2$, so integration gives $M(A)=A^{-1}$ with tail norm
at most $1/(\lambda_{\min}(A)+S)$.

For the matrix path, continuity provides a uniform local lower spectral
bound $m=\lambda_{\min}(A_0)/2$, while continuous differentiability gives
a local bound $K$ on $\|A'(t)\|$. The ordered derivative
$-R A'R^2-R^2A'R$ has norm at most $2K/(m+s)^3$, whose integral is
$K/m^2$. Thus differentiating through the improper integral is justified
locally, not merely asserted after evaluating the integral.

At $t_0$ those ordered terms are the $s$ derivative of $R_0 H R_0$;
their integral is $-A_0^{-1}HA_0^{-1}$. No step commutes $H$ through a
resolvent. The eigenbasis check $-\widetilde H_{ij}/(\lambda_i\lambda_j)$
correctly detects the tempting but false $-A_0^{-2}H$ replacement. The
zero- and negative-eigenvalue exclusions are also justified by actual poles.

Fresh checks quadrature full two-by-two resolvent squares for a positive
matrix and a symmetric perturbation that does not commute with it. Finite
differences of those integrals agree with the ordered derivative and
distinguish it from the invalid reordered formula.

**Finding:** convergence, local domination, product order, and the result
all agree. No correction required.

## I4-010: an oriented moving Gaussian

**Finding:** both differentiation routes, orientation, Gaussian mass, and

## I4-011: circular aperture normalization and remainder

The compact aperture has area $\pi a^2$ and an integrand of magnitude one.
Angular reflection cancels its imaginary part and makes the transform even
in signed $q$. Integrating the uniformly convergent exponential series,
using the even cosine moment
$2\pi(2m)!/(4^m(m!)^2)$, gives the stated local $J_0$ series. Radial
integration then produces the $J_1$ series normalization and
$F(q;a)=2\pi aJ_1(qa)/q$ away from zero. Since $J_1(z)=z/2+O(z^3)$,
the removable value is the original disk area, not twice that area.

The oddness of $J_1$ cancels the denominator's sign; introducing an absolute
value into only one factor would be wrong. For the quadratic approximation,
the constant and second angular moments give
$\pi a^2-\pi a^4q^2/8$. The pointwise real-cosine remainder is nonnegative
and at most its argument's fourth power divided by 24. Integrating it gives
$0\le E\le\pi a^6q^4/192$, using the fourth angular moment $3\pi/4$
and radial moment $a^6/6$. Both the absolute constant and the relative bound
$(qa)^4/192$ are correct and dimensionally consistent. The bound is valid
for all real $q$; only its usefulness as a small-parameter approximation
depends on small $|qa|$.

Fresh original angular/radial quadrature checks both signs of the wavenumber,
zero, realness, oscillatory values, the independently coded defining Bessel
series, and the signed remainder bound.

**Finding:** Bessel normalization, parity, removable value, convergence
justifications, and remainder are correct. No correction required.
