## Statement-first expected results

- **D4-001.** Write $f=\exp u$ with
  $u=\arctan(x)\ln(\ln(1+x^2))$. The derivative is
  $f(x)(\ln(\ln(1+x^2))+2x\arctan(x)/\ln(1+x^2))/(1+x^2)$ on
  $\mathbb R\setminus\{0\}$. The base is positive throughout that punctured
  domain. Since $u=2x\ln|x|+O(x^3|\ln|x||+|x|^3)$, the continuous extension
  is $f(0)=1$, and its difference quotient tends to $-\infty$ from either side.
  There is no finite derivative at zero.
- **D4-002.** Implicitly differentiate $\cos\theta=\tanh x$. The selected
  interval makes $\sin\theta=1/\cosh x>0$, hence
  $d\theta/dx=-1/\cosh x$ and $d^2\theta/dx^2=\sinh x/\cosh^2x$ for all
  finite real $x$. The alternate representation $2\arctan(\exp(-x))$ checks
  the selected branch.
- **D4-003.** The two positive radical factors have product one. Thus
  $f(x)=2\ln(\sqrt{1+x^2}+x)=2\operatorname{arsinh}x$ on all real inputs,
  with derivative $2/\sqrt{1+x^2}$. For negative inputs, the equivalent
  $-2\ln(\sqrt{1+x^2}-x)$ avoids the cancellation on that side.
- **D4-004.** Put $D(x,y)=2-x^2-2y^2$. The selected height is
  $g=(-x+\sqrt D)/2$ on $D>0$. The partials are
  $-1/2-x/(2\sqrt D)$ and $-y/\sqrt D$. Along the prescribed path $D=1$,
  so $g=(1-\cos t)/2$ and the path derivative is $\sin t/2$. The full
  level surface is regular even where this height chart ends.
- **D4-005.** A continuous phase with the prescribed normalization is
  $\phi=2\arctan(\omega\tau)-\omega T$. Its derivative is
  $2\tau/(1+\omega^2\tau^2)-T$. Negative delay occurs for all finite
  frequencies when $T=0$, for
  $|\omega|<\sqrt{2\tau/T-1}/\tau$ when $0<T<2\tau$, and never when
  $T\ge2\tau$. The equality case $T=2\tau,\omega=0$ gives zero delay.
  A principal-phase derivative agrees on each continuous phase chart and
  fails at any genuine jump; the $T=0$ phase has no finite-frequency jump.
- **D4-006.** The Leibniz rule gives the upper term
  $\exp(t)\exp(-t\exp(2t))/(1+\exp(2t))$, the lower term
  $-2t\exp(-t^5)/(1+t^4)$, and the integral of
  $-s^2\exp(-ts^2)/(1+s^2)$. At zero the derivative is $\pi/4-1/2$.
  Compact local rectangles justify the calculation for every finite real
  parameter, including coinciding or reversed endpoints.
- **D4-007.** With $h=\sin(1/x)\ln(1+x^2)$, $|h|\le x^2$. Hence the unique
  continuous extension has value one and derivative zero at the origin.
  On the punctured line,
  $df/dx=f(2x\sin(1/x)/(1+x^2)-\ln(1+x^2)\cos(1/x)/x^2)$.
  The derivative equals $-\cos(1/x)+o(1)$, giving subsequential limits
  $-1$ and $1$ along reciprocal even and odd multiples of $\pi$.
- **D4-008.** For $F(x,y)=x\exp y+y\exp x-1$, $F_y(0,1)=1$ proves a unique
  local smooth branch. At the base point, $F_x=e+1$, $F_{xx}=1$,
  $F_{xy}=e+1$, and $F_{yy}=0$. Thus $dg/dx=-(e+1)$ and
  $d^2g/dx^2=2(e+1)^2-1$ at zero. The quadratic coefficient is
  $(e+1)^2-1/2$, and smoothness gives an $O(x^3)$ remainder. Substitution
  makes both the linear coefficient $e+1-(e+1)$ and the quadratic
  coefficient $1/2+((e+1)^2-1/2)-(e+1)^2$ vanish.
- **D4-009.** The maximizing coordinate is zero for $t\le0$,
  $(t/4)^{1/3}$ for $0<t<4$, and one for $t\ge4$. The values are
  $0$, $3(t/4)^{4/3}$, and $t-1$. The derivative is the maximizing
  coordinate, continuous also at zero and four. The interior second
  derivative is $(1/12)(t/4)^{-2/3}$; outside the middle interval it is
  zero. At zero there is no finite second derivative; at four its
  one-sided values $1/12$ and zero disagree. The value function is $C^1$
  but not twice differentiable at either transition.
- **D4-010.** The identity $\mathbf A(t)^2=-t\mathbf I$ separates the
  exponential into $C(t)\mathbf I+S(t)\mathbf A(t)$ with entire series
  $C=\sum(-t)^m/(2m)!$ and $S=\sum(-t)^m/(2m+1)!$. These become cosine
  and sine-over-argument for positive $t$, and hyperbolic cosine and
  hyperbolic sine-over-argument for negative $t$. The derivative entries
  are $-S/2$, $dS/dt$, $-(C+S)/2$, $-S/2$, where
  $dS/dt=(C-S)/(2t)$ off zero and $dS/dt=-1/6$ at zero. The derivative
  at zero is $[[-1/2,-1/6],[-1,-1/2]]$. Multiplying the exponential at
  zero by the matrix derivative instead gives $[[-1,0],[-1,0]]$, so the
  scalar-inspired rule fails.
- **D4-011.** The open domain is $a>0$, $d=ac-b^2>0$. Since
  $f=\ln d+(a+c)/d$, the derivatives are
  $c/d-(c^2+b^2)/d^2$,
  $-2b/d+2b(a+c)/d^2$, and
  $a/d-(a^2+b^2)/d^2$. If the two off-diagonal entries vary independently,
  each contributes half of the symmetric-parameter derivative at the
  symmetric matrix. The entry gradient is
  $\mathbf A^{-1}-\mathbf A^{-2}$ there, with this transpose convention
  valid because the evaluation matrix is symmetric.

### D4-001

The logarithmic derivative includes both the base and exponent contributions,
and the inner derivative $2x/(1+x^2)$ is correct. Each nonzero real input has a
strictly positive logarithmic base; both signs of $x$ are admissible. The local
expansion is valid on both sides: factoring $x^2$ from $\ln(1+x^2)$ gives
$2\ln|x|+O(x^2)$ for its logarithm. Multiplication by
$x+O(x^3)$ gives exactly the two remainder terms in Step 4. In Step 5,
the square of this logarithmic output is $O(x^2\ln^2|x|)$, whose exponential
remainder after division by $x$ is $O(|x|\ln^2|x|)=o(1)$.
Thus the displayed $2\ln|x|+o(1)$ difference quotient is justified, including
on negative inputs, rather than inferred by differentiating an error term.
Step 6 correctly says that the second contribution to the logarithmic derivative
tends to two, while the first tends to negative infinity. All hints agree.
Adversarial cases checked: positive and negative small inputs, the base value
one at $x^2=e-1$, and the excluded zero input. No finding.

### D4-002

The selected inverse-cosine interval is essential: its sine is positive, so the
square root of $1-\tanh^2x$ equals $1/\cosh x$ without a missing absolute
value. Both displayed derivatives match direct differentiation of
$2\arctan(\exp(-x))$. Denominators never vanish at a finite real input.
The values and parity at zero are correct; the limiting angles at the two
infinities are excluded from the finite-input range. The hints and the sign of
the first derivative are consistent. Adversarial cases checked: large positive
and negative inputs conceptually, and the central point where the second
derivative changes sign. No finding.

### D4-003

The radical factors are strictly positive even for large negative inputs, and
their product is one. Thus reducing the logarithmic ratio to twice a logarithm
does not use an unjustified logarithmic rule. The subsequent cancellation divides
by a positive nonzero factor and yields the correct derivative on all real
inputs. The alternate negative-input expression follows by the same reciprocal
identity and correctly avoids that side's small difference. The source problem
requests avoidance of cancellation for large positive inputs, not a claim that
the expression prevents floating-point overflow at arbitrary machine values.
The inverse-hyperbolic check and hints agree. Adversarial cases checked: zero,
both signs of the input, and positive/negative large magnitudes. No finding.

### D4-004

The discriminant and selected sign give the stated height exactly, and the
strict branch condition is equivalent to $D>0$. Both implicit partials agree
with differentiating the explicit height. The path radicand is identically
one for every real parameter, so neither a branch crossing nor a boundary

cancel exactly as shown. At $D=0$, the branch condition itself fails, so no

### D4-005
